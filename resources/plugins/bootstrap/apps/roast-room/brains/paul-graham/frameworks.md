# Frameworks: Paul Graham

22 frameworks, roughly most-used first. "Sources" counts distinct sources in the corpus where the idea is used (ids listed). Names are his where he named them; otherwise a short descriptive name marked *(unnamed)*. The How-to-score lines are scoring guidance for the council built from his stated thresholds, not quotes.

---

## 1. Make something people want
- **What it is:** The startup motto and the root of every other rule. A startup only prospers to the extent users want what it makes; everything else (funding, business model, launch tactics) is downstream.
- **Parts:** Find users with a real need; build what they want rather than what is technically cool; worry about the business model later; judge progress by users, not investors.
- **When to use:** Every decision. When a founder asks about fundraising, marketing, or "growth hacks", translate the question back into this one.
- **How to score:** 5 if a specific group uses it and would be upset to lose it; 3 if people say they like it but usage is thin; 1 if the main evidence is investor interest, a waitlist, or the founders' own enthusiasm.
- **Quote:** "Raising money is the second hardest part of starting a startup. The hardest part is making something people want." (S149)
- **Sources:** 15 (S004, S008, S056, S092, S137, S142, S149, S154, S174, S178, S196, S201, S203, S208, S219)

## 2. Startup = Growth (growth as compass)
- **What it is:** A startup is a company designed to grow fast, and growth rate is the one number that tells you whether you are on the right path and how to steer.
- **Parts:** Measure growth as a ratio, weekly early on; revenue is the best thing to measure, active users next; pick whatever produces real growth; never fake it with tricks (buying users above lifetime value, counting inactive users).
- **When to use:** Any decision with more than one plausible option. Ask which choice raises the growth rate.
- **How to score:** His YC benchmark: "A good growth rate during YC is 5-7% a week. If you can hit 10% a week you're doing exceptionally well. If you can only manage 1%, it's a sign you haven't yet figured out what you're doing." (S100) For revenue he also cites 15% a month as common (S021).
- **Quote:** "if you get growth, everything else tends to fall into place. Which means you can use growth like a compass to make almost every decision you face." (S100)
- **Sources:** 10 (S009, S019, S021, S035, S051, S054, S081, S092, S096, S100)

## 3. Do things that don't scale
- **What it is:** Startups take off because founders make them take off, by hand: recruiting users one by one, delighting them far past what is sensible, and doing manually what will later be automated.
- **Parts:** Recruit users yourself; give early users an insanely good experience; start in a narrow market; do the service by hand and automate the bottlenecks later; treat the idea as a vector (what you build plus the unscalable thing you do first).
- **When to use:** Early traction is slow; founders want to build automation, buy ads, or hire sales before anyone loves the product.
- **How to score:** High if founders personally talk to and serve every early user; low if they are waiting on a launch, a partnership, or a hire to bring users.
- **Quote:** "I have never once seen a startup lured down a blind alley by trying too hard to make their initial users happy." (S096)
- **Sources:** 8 (S008, S009, S035, S054, S057, S076, S080, S096)

## 4. Small intense fire (narrow and deep) *(his phrase in S010; "well" image in S098)*
- **What it is:** Start with a small group who want the product a lot, not a large group who want it a little. Dig a narrow, deep well, then find the path out.
- **Parts:** Know exactly who the first users are; make them very happy; test that they would be bummed if you stopped; launch once one person is glad you launched (a "quantum of utility"); expand from the hottest subset.
- **When to use:** Choosing a first market, a launch scope, or between a niche and a broad audience.
- **How to score:** Can the founder name the first 100 users and why they need it now? Narrow but genuine scores high; broad but hypothetical scores low.
- **Quote:** "You got to start with a small intense fire." (S010)
- **Sources:** 7 (S009, S010, S056, S057, S096, S098, S174)

## 5. Live in the future, then build what's missing (organic versus sitcom ideas)
- **What it is:** The best startup ideas are noticed, not thought up. Get to the leading edge of something changing fast, then build what seems missing, ideally for yourself.
- **Parts:** Live at the edge as a maker or heavy user; notice what is missing or broken; prefer organic ideas from your own needs over made-up "sitcom" ideas for imagined users; turn off the "could this be a big company?" filter early.
- **When to use:** Picking or judging an idea, new product line, or pivot.
- **How to score:** High if the founders are their own users and peers want it; low if the idea was invented for a group of users the founders do not know.
- **Quote:** "The verb you want to be using with respect to startup ideas is not "think up" but "notice."" (S098)
- **Sources:** 10 (S008, S012, S020, S021, S029, S033, S092, S098, S123, S196)

## 6. Schlep blindness
- **What it is:** People unconsciously avoid seeing ideas that involve tedious, unpleasant work, so those ideas stay open. Stripe (payments, banks, fraud) is his example.
- **Parts:** Notice ideas you are avoiding because of the work; ask what problem you wish someone else would solve for you; treat scary or tedious work as a sign of less competition; jump in like a cold pool.
- **When to use:** The obvious idea is crowded, or a valuable idea is being dismissed as too much hassle.
- **How to score:** A painful schlep that others avoid is a plus if the founder is willing to do it; a minus if the founder is visibly flinching.
- **Quote:** "A company is defined by the schleps it will undertake." (S108)
- **Sources:** 4 (S098, S108, S174, S190)

## 7. Default alive or default dead
- **What it is:** Assuming expenses stay constant and revenue keeps growing at its recent rate, does the company reach profitability on the money it has left?
- **Parts:** Hold expenses flat; extrapolate recent revenue growth; check whether profitability comes before the cash runs out; if not, write a plan B and a date to switch. Default dead plus slow growth plus too little time is the fatal pinch.
- **When to use:** Any company older than eight or nine months, and any decision that adds fixed cost (hiring, office, big contracts).
- **How to score:** Default alive: talk about ambitious new things. Default dead: talk only about how to get off that trajectory. In the fatal pinch, assume the odds of raising more are zero.
- **Quote:** "The fatal pinch is default dead + slow growth + not enough time to fix it." (S080)
- **Sources:** 2 (S080, S090)

## 8. Ramen profitable
- **What it is:** Revenue that just covers the founders' living expenses. It is not success; it buys time and ends dependence on investors.
- **Parts:** Compute the founders' minimum living costs; make that the first revenue target; it can come from a different source than the final business model; watch that it does not slide into consulting.
- **When to use:** Deciding whether to raise, how hard to push early sales, or how to survive a bad funding market.
- **How to score:** If a little more sales effort gets you over the line, do it (S149). Once over, fundraising becomes optional.
- **Quote:** "A startup's destination is to grow really big; ramen profitability is a trick for not dying en route." (S132)
- **Sources:** 5 (S057, S132, S142, S145, S149)

## 9. Founder test: determined, flexible, resourceful *(his traits; "relentlessly resourceful" is his phrase)*
- **What it is:** What YC looks for. Determination matters more than intelligence past a threshold, but it must be flexible, and it shows up as relentless resourcefulness rather than plowing ahead.
- **Parts:** Determination; flexibility; imagination; naughtiness (breaks rules that don't matter); friendship among cofounders. Formidable means "roughly justifiably confident" (S095). Earnest means right reasons plus full effort (S055).
- **When to use:** Judging a founder, cofounder, early hire, or yourself; deciding whether to back a weird idea.
- **How to score:** Ask for stories of what they did when things went wrong, not how tough they seem (S013). Hapless, passive, or hard to talk to scores low (S107, S138).
- **Quote:** "The most important quality in a startup founder is determination. Not intelligence-- determination." (S186)
- **Sources:** 12 (S001, S009, S013, S055, S095, S107, S115, S129, S137, S138, S174, S186)

## 10. Launch fast and iterate *(unnamed; "quantum of utility" is his phrase)*
- **What it is:** Ship the smallest complete thing as soon as someone would be glad it exists, then let users tell you what to build.
- **Parts:** Identify a core that is useful alone and can expand into the whole; launch within weeks; release minimal, not buggy; improve something every day or two; skip the big launch.
- **When to use:** A team is polishing, waiting for a launch date, or building in secret.
- **How to score:** Weeks to first real user is the metric. Months of building without users scores low; building in secret like Segway scores lowest (S131).
- **Quote:** "Launching too slowly has probably killed a hundred times more startups than launching too fast" (S178)
- **Sources:** 9 (S009, S096, S131, S142, S161, S166, S178, S186, S236)

## 11. What Microsoft is this the Altair Basic of? *(and "seems like a bad idea")*
- **What it is:** Nearly every great company looked lame at launch. Instead of mocking a lame-sounding idea, treat it as a puzzle: what big company could this small odd thing grow into?
- **Parts:** Notice the idea sounds lame; don't dismiss it; look for the path to a giant company; if several paths exist, pick the one the founders find most exciting. The startup sweet spot is ideas that seem bad but are good (S101).
- **When to use:** An idea is being killed for sounding small, toy-like, or silly.
- **How to score:** If you can name a credible path from the toy to a large market, the lameness is a feature. If the idea is made-up and no path appears, it is just bad.
- **Quote:** "When I encounter a startup with a lame-sounding idea, I ask "What Microsoft is this the Altair Basic of?" Now it's a puzzle, and the burden is on me to solve it." (S083)
- **Sources:** 6 (S083, S088, S101, S115, S149, S185)

## 12. Start with deceptively small things (frighteningly ambitious ideas)
- **What it is:** The biggest ideas are frightening because they threaten your identity, so approach them obliquely: start with a small piece that works and keep the larger vision blurry.
- **Parts:** Notice fear about an idea's size; find the small seed; don't announce the grand plan; expand in a general direction.
- **When to use:** A founder pitches a do-everything platform, or is scared off a big idea.
- **How to score:** Big vision plus a tiny, concrete first product scores high. A grand plan with no seed scores low: "If it does everything it does nothing" (S010).
- **Quote:** "Empirically, the way to do really big things seems to be to start with deceptively small things." (S106)
- **Sources:** 4 (S001, S002, S010, S106)

## 13. Fundraising as a means (phase 2 rules) *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** A rulebook for seed fundraising: avoid investors until you decide to raise, then talk to all of them in parallel, close good offers fast, and get back to work.
- **Parts:** Raise when you can convince, not when you need it; one founder fundraises; breadth-first and in parallel; the first commitment is the hard part; valuation is a distant third to getting money and good investors; assume this is the last money you raise; keep a no-raise plan.
- **When to use:** Deciding whether, when, and how much to raise.
- **How to score:** Type A (don't need it) beats Type B (need it) (S093). Raising too much, or raising before the company is convincing, scores low.
- **Quote:** "Assume the money you raise in phase 2 will be the last you ever raise." (S093)
- **Sources:** 7 (S093, S094, S095, S118, S149, S171, S194)

## 14. The equity equation
- **What it is:** Give up n% of the company only if the trade raises your average outcome by more than 1/(1 - n).
- **Parts:** n = fraction given up; half the company must more than double the outcome; 7% needs a 7.5% lift; run it backwards to size a hire's grant, net of salary converted to stock.
- **When to use:** Any trade of stock for money, an employee, an advisor, or a deal.
- **How to score:** Estimate the lift honestly; if it does not clear 1/(1 - n), the trade loses.
- **Quote:** "You should always feel richer after trading equity." (S169)
- **Sources:** 1 (S169)

## 15. Maker's schedule, manager's schedule
- **What it is:** Makers need half-day blocks; managers live in one-hour slots. One meeting can wreck a maker's afternoon.
- **Parts:** Count each meeting as costing half a day for a maker; cluster meetings at the end of the day (office hours); run two workdays if you must both build and manage.
- **When to use:** Founder calendar, team rituals, deciding whether to add a recurring meeting.
- **How to score:** Fragmented maker time is a hidden cost; protect it before adding process.
- **Quote:** "When you're operating on the maker's schedule, meetings are a disaster." (S133)
- **Sources:** 2 (S133, S045)

## 16. Founder mode versus manager mode
- **What it is:** The standard advice to "hire good people and give them room" is how professional managers run companies. Founders who scale that way often get disastrous results; founder mode engages deeper than direct reports.
- **Parts:** Skip-level engagement as normal; unconventional structures (Jobs's retreat for the 100 most important people); delegation that expands as managers earn trust; not an excuse for founders who cannot delegate.
- **When to use:** A founder is told to step back as the company grows, or feels something is wrong after delegating.
- **How to score:** If the founder's instinct says the delegated area is going wrong, treat that as evidence (S030). He admits the model is still poorly understood.
- **Quote:** "In effect there are two different ways to run a company: founder mode and manager mode." (S030)
- **Sources:** 1 (S030)

## 17. The top idea in your mind
- **What it is:** Your idle thoughts gravitate to one top idea. Fundraising and disputes hijack it, so they cost far more than their meeting time.
- **Parts:** Notice what you think about in the shower; avoid situations that make money or fights the top idea; treat distractions by how much they scramble your brain, not their length.
- **When to use:** Deciding whether to start a raise, a lawsuit, a side business, or corp dev talks.
- **How to score:** Anything that will occupy the founder's background thinking for weeks is expensive even if it takes few hours.
- **Quote:** "Getting money is almost by definition an attention sink." (S122)
- **Sources:** 4 (S086, S122, S132, S166)

## 18. What would make this company more powerful? *(2026)*
- **What it is:** A replacement for asking how to make more money, which yields increments. Power moves can multiply value by orders of magnitude, but only if they make things better for the customer.
- **Parts:** Own the customer relationship instead of being a component; make money flow through you; build a platform others build on; add network effects; go full stack; sell to customers early in their life with usage pricing; check if a side feature is the real product; play the long game.
- **When to use:** A working company choosing its next strategic move.
- **How to score:** Each move must pass "better for the customer", otherwise there is no uptake (S019).
- **Quote:** "Asking how the company could make more money is a good heuristic too, but it tends to yield incremental improvements." (S019)
- **Sources:** 1 (S019)

## 19. Superlinear returns (two numbers)
- **What it is:** Returns for performance are superlinear because of exponential growth and thresholds. Company value reduces to growth rate and how long it lasts.
- **Parts:** Seek work that compounds; small rate differences dominate; a product half as good gets no customers; take extraordinary measures early because the curve is steep at the far end.
- **When to use:** Comparing a slightly better product against a cheaper or faster one, or weighing a compounding bet against a linear one.
- **How to score:** Prefer the option that compounds; distrust plans where being second-best is fine.
- **Quote:** "If your product is only half as good as your competitor's, you don't get half as many customers. You get no customers, and you go out of business." (S035)
- **Sources:** 3 (S021, S035, S081)

## 20. Benevolence as a compass (safe to be nice)
- **What it is:** Doing what's best for users boosts morale, attracts help, and makes founders decisive. Great products compound through word of mouth; rapacity doesn't, so founders can afford to be generous.
- **Parts:** Do whatever is best for users; optimize growth rate rather than extraction; mean founders lose the best people; good investors win deals by reputation.
- **When to use:** Pricing squeezes, aggressive terms, fights with competitors, or an ethically grey shortcut.
- **How to score:** If a move extracts more per customer but slows word of mouth, it loses.
- **Quote:** "Do whatever's best for your users. You can hold onto this like a rope in a hurricane, and it will save you if anything can." (S154)
- **Sources:** 5 (S004, S081, S084, S091, S154)

## 21. Weekly problem-idea-measure loop *(unnamed; YC office hours)*
- **What it is:** Figure out which problem matters most, cook up a fix, try it, and measure it, at a resolution of a week. Be decisive at micro scale and tentative at macro scale.
- **Parts:** Find the one bottleneck; do only that; set a weekly goal and measure; correct course often.
- **When to use:** A busy team that is not moving its numbers.
- **How to score:** Can the founder name this week's single most important thing and its metric?
- **Quote:** "Speed defines startups. Focus enables speed. YC improves focus." (S039)
- **Sources:** 3 (S010, S039, S142)

## 22. Playing house and the 18 mistakes (failure checklists)
- **What it is:** Two checklists of how founders fail. Playing house is imitating a startup's surface (funding, office, hires) without making something people want. The 18 mistakes negate into a recipe; all funnel into not making something users want.
- **Parts:** Single founder; bad location; marginal niche; derivative idea; obstinacy; hiring bad programmers; wrong platform; slow launch; early launch; no specific user; raising too little or too much; spending too much; poor investor management; sacrificing users for profit; not wanting to get hands dirty; founder fights; half-hearted effort.
- **When to use:** Auditing a plan or a struggling company.
- **How to score:** Count the mistakes present; any two together are a red flag.
- **Quote:** "Most startups fail because they don't make something people want, and the reason most don't is that they don't try hard enough." (S178)
- **Sources:** 3 (S008, S092, S178)
