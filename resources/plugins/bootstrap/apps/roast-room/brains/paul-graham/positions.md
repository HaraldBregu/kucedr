# Positions: Paul Graham

36 topics. Each stance has a verbatim quote, source id, and date (essay month, or YouTube upload date for talks; see `sources.md`). Where he changed his mind or shifted emphasis, the versions sit side by side with dates. Topics with no evidence say so.

## Raising venture money
- **Stance:** Raise to choose your growth rate, never as the test of success, and act as if it is the last money you will get. Fundraising is a necessary evil.
- **Over time:**
  - 2005-03: "I think it's wise to take money from investors." (S208) Self-funding meant starting as a consulting company.
  - 2008-12: cheap startups can skip VC: "many Internet startups don't need VC-scale investments anymore." (S145)
  - 2012-09: "Raising money lets you choose your growth rate." (S100) The most successful take it even when they don't need it.
  - 2026-08: "Fundraising is merely a necessary evil. The people you need to impress are users, not investors" (S020)
- **Read:** consistent core (product first, money as means), but he swings between taking investment and not needing it as the cost of starting up changes.

## How much to raise and valuation
- **Stance:** Raise enough to reach the next visible milestone, don't fix one number, and don't fight over valuation. "The most important thing to understand about valuation is that it's not that important." (S093, 2013-09) "The more you raise, the more you spend, and spending a lot of money can be disastrous for an early stage startup." (S093, 2013-09)
- **Earlier:** a high valuation narrows your exits and is not value in itself: "But you can't eat paper." (S207, 2005-03)

## Angels versus VCs
- **Stance:** Prefers good angels and smaller rounds with less dilution and more control. "The quality of investor is more important than the type of round, though." (S120, 2010-08) "The best thing for founders, if they can get it, is a convertible note with no valuation cap at all." (S116, 2010-10)

## Fundraising in a hot market
- **2014:** "prices are high raise money now" (S006, interview uploaded 2014-09). He refused to call it a bubble: "Valuations are high that's not the same thing as a bubble." (S010, 2014-03)
- **2026:** surprised by price levels: "demo day valuations are like $60 million now, right?" (S012, uploaded 2026-03)

## Debt
- **Stance:** Against debt-financing a startup. "Do not finance your startup with credit cards. Financing a startup with debt is usually a stupid move, and credit card debt stupidest of all." (S158, 2008-03) Convertible notes he treats as equity-like instruments, not loans (S138, 2009-03).

## Ramen profitability and bootstrapping
- **Stance:** Get to ramen profitable early to buy time, but it is not the goal and does not mean refusing investment. "It does not, for example, imply that you're "bootstrapping" the startup" (S132, 2009-07) "Once you cross the threshold of profitability, however low, your runway becomes infinite." (S145, 2008-12)

## Pricing
- **Stance:** Don't agonize; price to get customers and growth, change it later, and grandfather existing users. "you can always change your prices later though if you want to lower your prices no one's gonna complain" (S009, uploaded 2018-09)
- **Over time:**
  - 2005-11: "we tell startups that they should try to make as little money as possible." (S193)
  - 2015-08: under-charging only costs a constant factor; "If you're going to optimize a number, the one to choose is your growth rate." (S081)
  - 2026-09: "I generally advise startups to sell as cheaply as they need to at first; get all the users, then worry about your margins." (S019) He adds usage-based pricing for customers you sign early (S019).

## Discounting
- **No recorded position** on discounts as a sales tactic. Nearest: sell cheaply at first, then fix margins (S019, 2026-09), and squeezing customers "gets you 2x returns at most" (S019).

## When to think about the business model
- **2006-10:** "In version 1, solve the core problem." (S178)
- **2008-07:** "Your company has to make money." (S150) Charge early if selling helps you understand users.
- **2009-07:** disagrees with putting the business model in beta from day one; "a ramen profitable company doesn't have to be making money the way it ultimately will." (S132)
- **Read:** product first, but profitability is never optional; he got firmer about making money from 2008 on.

## First hire and hiring pace
- **Stance:** Hire as late and as few as possible; hire only what the founders can't do.
- **Over time:**
  - 2005-03: "I may be an extremist, but I think hiring people is the worst thing a company can do." (S208)
  - 2015-10: "Hiring too fast is by far the biggest killer of startups that raise money." (S080) Airbnb "waited 4 months after raising money at the end of Y Combinator before they hired their first employee." (S080)
  - 2024-09: the standard scaling advice to hire good people and give them room is how "to run a company if you're merely a professional manager" (S030)

## Firing
- **Stance:** In a crisis, cut people first, starting with the ones you already know about. "there's one case in which it shouldn't be: when there are people you already know you should fire but you're in denial about it." (S090, 2014-12) "you're not doing those 15 people any favors if you fly the company into ground with them aboard." (S090, 2014-12)

## Cofounders and solo founders
- **Stance:** Get a cofounder before almost anything else.
- **Over time:**
  - 2005-10: YC "has a rule against investing in startups with only one founder" (S196)
  - 2014-03: "I don't recommend it though get a co-founder." (S010)
  - 2023-11: softer: "it's harder I know I wouldn't start a startup alone" (S013)
- **Equity split:** "it's usually better to have somewhat equal shares" but not 50/50 deadlock (S009, uploaded 2018-09). Vest so a cofounder can leave cleanly (S178, 2006-10).

## Equity trades
- **Stance:** Run every trade through 1/(1 - n). "You should always feel richer after trading equity." (S169, 2007-07)

## Partnerships and big-company deals
- **Stance:** Don't count on them. "Partnerships too usually don't work." (S096, 2013-07) Ideas that need deals with incumbents just to launch won't happen (S032, 2024-03).

## Selling the company
- **Over time:**
  - 2004-05: "I think it's a good idea to get bought, if you can." (S219)
  - 2008-04: "turning down reasonable offers is the most reliable test you could invent for whether a startup will make it big." (S155)
  - 2008-07: selling is the best case of living off revenue, if you are profitable enough to wait: "Being profitable ensures you'll get at least the average of the acquisition market." (S150)
  - 2014-11: "The ones driven by money take the big acquisition offer that nearly every successful startup gets en route." (S091)
- **Read:** from selling if you can (his own Viaweb exit) to treating early sale as a sign of a money-driven founder. Both views agree: profitability gives you the choice.

## Talking to acquirers (corp dev)
- **Stance:** Only if you want to sell now, which means doing very well or very badly. "They want to talk about buying you. That's what the title "corp dev" means." (S086, 2015-01)

## Focus versus diversification
- **Stance:** One thing at a time; find the bottleneck. "The number one thing not to do is other things." (S166, 2007-08) "There is something that is currently the bottleneck to making the software faster you work on that." (S010, 2014-03)

## Side projects
- **Split by stage.** Before a startup, side projects are the best source of ideas: "The way to discover the best startup ideas is not to look for startup ideas but just to work on whatever random projects seem interesting." (S020, 2026-08) After you start, paying side projects are poison: "The worst type are those that pay money: day jobs, consulting, profitable side-projects." (S142, 2009-02)

## Consulting and services revenue
- **Over time:**
  - 2005-11: "Consulting is where product companies go to die." (S194)
  - 2008-08: the only reliable bootstrapping route, but chronic pain; raising is usually the lesser evil (S149).
  - 2014-12: in the fatal pinch, consulting-type work is a legitimate way to survive if you stay mercenary (S090).

## Niche versus big market
- **Stance:** Start in a narrow market that wants it badly, with a path out. Don't pick a small niche to hide from competition. "If you make anything good, you're going to have competitors, so you may as well face that." (S178, 2006-10) "A need that's narrow but genuine is a better starting point than one that's broad but hypothetical." (S174, 2007-03)

## Competition
- **Stance:** Rarely the threat it seems; a crowded market is a sign of demand. "Competitors are rarely what kills startups. Poor execution does." (S056, 2020-12) "better a good idea with competitors than a bad one without." (S098, 2012-11) When one appears: "What should you do you run as fast as you can" (S009)

## Launch and marketing
- **Stance:** Big launches rarely matter; recruit users by hand and let word of mouth work. "I should mention one sort of initial tactic that usually doesn't work: the Big Launch." (S096, 2013-07) "On the Internet, anything genuinely good will spread by word of mouth." (S201, 2005-05)

## Paid ads
- **Stance:** Skeptical for differentiated products. "spending a lot on brand advertising is a sign of breakage." (S208, 2005-03) Never buy users for more than their lifetime value (S100, 2012-09). Concedes that for products that aren't technically novel, deals and marketing matter more (S125, 2009-10).

## PR and press
- **Stance:** PR works and favors small companies; press only matters if your target users see it. Viaweb spent its marketing budget on PR (S202, 2005-04). "For something like YC, the only kind of out there that you care about is whether potential applicants know about you." (S012, uploaded 2026-03)

## Content marketing
- **No recorded position** on content as a growth channel for a company. He writes essays himself and treats writing as thinking (S028, 2024-10), but never prescribes content marketing to founders in the corpus.

## Brand
- **Stance:** Stay away from brand, both buying and selling it. "Brand is what's left when the substantive differences between products disappear." (S023, 2026-03) Consistent with 2005: brand advertising signals breakage except for undifferentiated products like vodka or detergent (S208, 2005-03).

## AI
- **2024-10:** "AI has blown this world open. Almost all pressure to write has dissipated." (S028)
- **2026:** startup basics are unchanged: "so far almost everything is exactly the same." The new thing is large AI bills (S001, uploaded 2026-09). "However good AI gets at being intelligent, it won't replace all the human founders because it doesn't have the needs that lead to startup ideas." (S012, uploaded 2026-03)
- **Timing worry:** to founders saying it will be too late: "People said that 2 years ago. Were they right? Is it too late now?" (S012)

## Remote work
- **No recorded position** on remote teams. Nearest evidence leans in-person: "things have got to happen in person" (S002, 2012-03), and hubs work because of people, not infrastructure (S110, 2011-10). Inferred lean: skeptical of fully remote early teams (inferred from S002, S110, S162).

## Location and startup hubs
- **Over time:**
  - 2006-10: "Places that aren't startup hubs are toxic to startups." (S179)
  - 2007-10: move to Silicon Valley, from Boston or London alike (S162).
  - 2026-05: softer: "You can go there for a bit and then come back, but you should at least go." (S003)

## Work hours and burnout
- **Stance:** Startups consume your life, but hard work has a ceiling. "I can't think of any successful startups whose founders worked 9 to 5." (S179, 2006-10) Later: "My limit for the harder types of writing or programming is about five hours a day. Whereas when I was running a startup, I could work all the time." (S045, 2021-06) "Work as hard as you can without burning out" (S035, 2023-10) After an exit, take a month or two off (S054, 2021-02).

## Culture
- **Stance:** Culture is set by early behavior: unscalable care for users, hacker-centric hiring, and cheapness. "A culture of cheapness keeps companies young in something like the way exercise keeps people young." (S142, 2009-02) Hire independent-minded people to slow conformity as you grow (S058, 2020-11).

## Metrics
- **Stance:** One number: growth rate, measured on revenue if you have it, active users if not. "If there's one number every founder should always know, it's the company's growth rate." (S100, 2012-09) Plot it daily on the wall (S142, 2009-02). Fundraising success is "positively misleading" as a metric (S101, 2012-09).

## Timing and bad economies
- **Stance:** The economy matters less than founders think. "Technology trains leave the station at regular intervals." (S148, 2008-10) Ask "why now?" by naming the change that made the idea newly possible (S095, 2013-08).

## Starting young and starting in college
- **Over time:**
  - 2006-10: "If I had to pick the sweet spot for startup founders, based on who we're most excited to see applications from, I'd say it's probably the mid-twenties." (S179)
  - 2008-03: revised: "ambitious programmers are better off doing their own thing and failing than going to work at a big company." (S158)
  - 2014-10: "You can't start a startup for real as a student, because if you start a startup for real you're not a student anymore." (S092)

## Founder control of the board
- **Stance:** Founders keeping control is good and becoming normal. "Founders retaining control after a series A is clearly heard-of." (S113, 2010-12) Scaling should not mean handing the company to managers (S030, 2024-09).

## Being nice versus ruthless
- **Stance:** Nice is safe and usually better. "Tell yourself you can be as nice as you want, so long as you work hard on your growth rate to compensate." (S081, 2015-08) "Bad people make bad founders." (S056, 2020-12)
