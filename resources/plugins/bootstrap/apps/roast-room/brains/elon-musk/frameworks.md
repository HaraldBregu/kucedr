# Frameworks: Elon Musk

Built from 39 sources (long interviews and podcasts, Starbase tours, TED talks, Tesla shareholder meetings and earnings calls, and SpaceX presentations, 2012 to 2026). 25 frameworks: engineering and execution first (1 to 16), then management and company building (17 to 25). Within each group, roughly most-used first, except the five-step algorithm, which leads because most of his other execution tools are pieces of it. "Sources" counts distinct sources that use the idea (ids listed). Names are his where he named them; otherwise a short name marked *(unnamed)*. The How-to-score lines are scoring guidance for the council built from his stated thresholds, not quotes.

## Engineering and execution

## 1. The algorithm (the five-step process)
- **What it is:** His ordered method for any part, process, or plan, which he says he is trying to have every team "implement rigorously" (S014). The order is the point: most waste comes from doing the steps backwards.
- **Steps:** (1) Make the requirements less dumb, and put a named person on each one. (2) Delete the part or process step. (3) Simplify or optimize, and only now. (4) Accelerate cycle time. (5) Automate, last.
- **When to use:** Any plan that is slow, costly, or complicated. Before a founder hires for a process, buys software for it, or automates it.
- **How to score:** 5 if requirements have owners, steps were deleted, and about 10% of deletions had to come back; 3 if the plan simplifies but deletes nothing; 1 if it speeds up or automates a process nobody questioned (his own Model 3 battery line mistake).
- **Quote:** "make your requirements less dumb. Your requirements are definitely dumb." (S014) and the deletion threshold: "if you're not adding things back in 10% of the time, you're clearly not deleting enough." (S014)
- **His own misuse:** "now I personally, uh, made the mistake of going backwards on all five steps multiple times." (S014)
- **Sources:** taught in full in 1 (S014); its steps (question, delete, make it work before optimizing, automate last) are applied in 9 more (S001, S006, S009, S011, S012, S017, S019, S034, S038). Total 10.

## 2. Tackle the limiting factor *(his phrase)*
- **What it is:** Find the one thing capping speed right now, solve it, then ask again. Money, people, and his own time follow the bottleneck; a project that is going well gets almost none of his attention.
- **Parts:** Pick a date or horizon (in 2026 he named power as the one-year limit and chips as the three-to-four-year limit, S002); name the single binding input; if nobody else will fix it, fix it yourself; predict the next bottleneck (silicon, then transformers, then electricity, S001); repeat.
- **When to use:** Any plan to go faster, a growth stall, or a question about where founder time should go.
- **How to score:** High if the plan names one binding constraint for a specific date and acts on it directly. Low if it works on five things at once, or on what is interesting instead of what caps speed.
- **Quote:** "I just repeatedly tackle the limiting factor. Whatever the limiting factor is on speed, I’m going to tackle that. If capital is the limiting factor, then I’ll solve for capital." (S002)
- **Second quote:** "You have to, like, sort of pick a point in time and say, what, what is the limiting factor at, at any given point in time." (S035)
- **Sources:** 12 (S001, S002, S013, S016, S027, S028, S030, S032, S033, S034, S035, S039)

## 3. Delete the part, delete the process (the best part is no part)
- **What it is:** The cheapest and most reliable part, step, or rule is the one that is gone. Anything not needed to solve the problem in front of you now is deleted or punted to later.
- **Parts:** Ask whether the item is necessary to solve the problem now (the Starship payload door, S009); move mass and work to the ground side (the tower catch instead of landing legs, S006, S009); merge many pieces into one (about 120 body pieces cast as one, S017); remove joints, flanges, and seals (S011); delete parts a new product makes vestigial (steering wheel and pedals, S019); delete legacy code slowly, because one bug can cover another (S019); keep deletions reversible.
- **When to use:** Feature lists, product scope, process steps, approval chains, and recurring costs.
- **How to score:** High if the plan lists what was removed and something had to be added back. Low if every item survives on an in-case argument.
- **Quote:** "is the door necessary to, uh, solve the problem? No." (S009) His shorthand: "best part being no part." (S034)
- **Sources:** 13 (S006, S009, S011, S012, S014, S015, S017, S019, S028, S030, S032, S034, S038)

## 4. Prototypes are easy, production is hard (the machine that builds the machine)
- **What it is:** Designing the product is the small job. Designing the system that makes it at volume is 10 to 100 times harder (S014), at least two orders of magnitude (S022), so the factory is the real product.
- **Parts:** Design the product and the factory together to a cycle time (a unit every five seconds versus 33 at the best line today, S034); linear adjacent flow, with every station given about the same work time (S011); test at the end of the line once yield is high and pull the debug tests out (S014); watch end-of-line output, because a stall means a blockage upstream (S029); keep management next to the factory (S030).
- **When to use:** Any physical product, and any plan that counts a prototype, demo, pilot, or launch event as the win. For services, the same test applies to the delivery system (inferred from S014, S024, S034).
- **How to score:** High if the plan has a delivery system with a cycle time and a cost per unit at volume. Low if success is defined as a working prototype, a press release, or a first customer.
- **Quote:** "I think generally manufacturing is underrated and design is overrated." (S014)
- **Second quote:** "the factory is the product as much as the car is the product." (S034)
- **Sources:** 14 (S006, S007, S008, S009, S011, S014, S022, S024, S028, S029, S032, S033, S034, S035)

## 5. First principles (reason up from physics, not by analogy)
- **What it is:** Boil a problem down to the facts most likely to be true, reason up from them, and cross-check the conclusion against physics. The default he rejects is copying what others do.
- **Parts:** List the axioms; check you are not violating physics (conservation of energy and momentum, S006); reason up; cross-check; copy the principle, not the form (submarines do not swim like fish, S007); when no catalog part exists, design it from physics (Optimus actuators, S002, S016, S039).
- **When to use:** An expert, supplier, or industry says something is impossible or must cost a certain amount.
- **How to score:** High if the plan names the physical or economic fact that actually limits it. Low if the only justification is that the industry does it this way.
- **Quote:** "break things down to the fundamental axiomatic elements that are most likely to be true, and then reason up from there as cogently as possible, as opposed to r-reasoning by analysis or metaphor." (S021)
- **Where it failed him:** Starship began in carbon fiber because that was the accepted answer, and progress was slow until he asked about steel: "The point is, in retrospect, we should have started with steel in the beginning. It was dumb not to do steel." (S002) He also moved Raptor from hydrogen to methane on test data (S006).
- **Sources:** 12 (S001, S002, S003, S006, S007, S016, S019, S021, S028, S032, S034, S039)

## 6. Build it yourself when the supply chain is too slow *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Vertical integration chosen by pace, not ideology. If suppliers cannot move as fast as you need, you inherit their speed, cost, and technology, so you build the input.
- **Parts:** Take everything suppliers can give; ask whether even their best case covers your need three or four years out (S039); if not, build it (chip team, Terafab, lithium refinery, transformers, labeling tools, rocket engines); guarantee to buy a supplier's output so they build faster (S002); fill your own demand before selling a scarce part to others (S039).
- **When to use:** A vendor's quote sets your schedule, or a critical input has one supplier.
- **How to score:** High if every key input has a plan B and capacity covers the next three years. Low if growth depends on a vendor's roadmap.
- **Quote:** "the pace at which we needed to move was just much faster than the supply chain could move." (S017)
- **His framing:** he says Tesla builds refineries "we build them out of desperation" (S039), meaning only after nobody else will.
- **Sources:** 13 (S001, S002, S005, S015, S016, S017, S027, S028, S032, S033, S034, S035, S039)

## 7. One metric for the whole company *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Choose the denominator before arguing. Each company gets one physical or unit-economic number that every team optimizes.
- **Parts:** His picks: cost per ton to orbit and to Mars (S006, S014, S038); useful payload to orbit per year, not launch count (S010); years by which Tesla sped up sustainable energy (S008, S025, S028); true user seconds instead of monetizable users (S031); unique real people instead of accounts (S017); fully considered cost per mile (S025, S039); gross profit per employee including the supply chain (S030); intelligence per gigabyte (S039); owner happiness over spec-sheet range (S008); life years lost instead of raw deaths (S007).
- **When to use:** A team is arguing about priorities, or a company reports a number that can be gamed.
- **How to score:** High if the metric is hard to fake and moves with real value. Low if it is a vanity count (accounts, launches, announcements).
- **Quote:** "The fundamental thing that's needs to be fixed is the, the cost per ton to orbit." (S014)
- **Second quote:** "Gross profit per employee, uh, f-fully considered, so you gotta include the supply chain in that." (S030)
- **Sources:** 12 (S006, S007, S008, S010, S014, S017, S025, S028, S030, S031, S038, S039)

## 8. Cost from raw materials (the magic wand number) and the million-units test
- **What it is:** Price a product from what its materials cost on the spot market. That is the floor; the gap to the actual price is how atoms are put into shape. Then ask whether low volume really explains the price.
- **Parts:** List the material constituents and price them (battery packs about $600 per kWh against about $80 of materials, S003); rocket raw materials were 1 to 2% of historical cost (S021); in volume, cost approaches materials plus IP royalties (S006, S015); the million-units test: if it is still expensive at a million a year, the design or process is the problem, not scale (S006); set the cost target at volume before setting price (Optimus about $20,000 at a million a year, S016, S033).
- **When to use:** Someone defends a cost or price with history or low volume.
- **How to score:** Guidance: the bigger the ratio of current cost to material floor, the more of the cost is process and the bigger the possible cut. A plan that cannot state its floor scores low. The name *idiot index* does not appear in this corpus, so agents should not quote it.
- **Quote:** "If it's still expensive at a million units a year, then volume is not the reason why your thing is expensive." (S006)
- **Second quote:** "what's actually causing things to be expensive is how you put the atoms into the desired shape." (S006)
- **Sources:** 6 (S003, S006, S015, S016, S021, S033)

## 9. The manufacturing S-curve and the least lucky part *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Output always starts agonizingly slow, then goes exponential, then linear, then flattens. A complex product ramps only as fast as the worst of its thousands of parts, so a product with no existing supply chain has a stretched S.
- **Parts:** Expect a shallow, bumpy start (S036, S010); walk the limiting part as it moves from one item to the next (S034); ramp rate equals how fast each problem gets solved (S034); you cannot predict the week-by-week slope, but you can know where the curve ends (S034).
- **When to use:** Forecasting a launch ramp, a new product's volume, or hiring and cash needs during a ramp.
- **How to score:** High if the forecast is a curve with a slow start and names its weakest parts. Low if it is a straight line from day one.
- **Quote:** "it moves as fast as the least lucky, dumbest element in the entire system, and there's ten thousand plus, uh, items." (S033)
- **Sources:** 6 (S002, S010, S033, S034, S036, S039)

## 10. Rapid iteration with a risk dial *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Set how much failure you accept by who gets hurt. Where no person or customer is at risk, iterate fast and let test units blow up; where people are aboard, be extremely conservative.
- **Parts:** Dragon allows no failures, Falcon some, Starship the opposite (S009); push test units into the corners of the box so real missions stay inside what is proven (S012); simulate thousands of runs and spend the effort on the failures (S012); the payload of a test flight is data (S011); test to destruction to learn where it breaks (S037); roll out to people slowly and remove safety scaffolding one layer at a time (S034, S039).
- **Why it matters to him:** the Shuttle program punished a failed change hard and rewarded a good one little, so known problems never got fixed (S009).
- **When to use:** Choosing the pace of experiments, launches, pilots, and rollouts.
- **How to score:** High if tests are fast and cheap where failure hurts nobody and cautious where customers carry the risk. Low if caution is uniform, or speed is applied where customers take the hit.
- **Quote:** "Starship does not have anyone on board, so we can blow things up." (S009)
- **Second quote:** "if you make a change and it goes right, small reward." (S009)
- **Sources:** 10 (S008, S009, S011, S012, S014, S018, S034, S035, S037, S039)

## 11. Make it work, then make it efficient (crawl, walk, run) *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Get one working version by any means, then optimize. New technology takes about three major versions to be excellent.
- **Parts:** Get one way at all that works (S011); build a small version and make the mistakes there, then the big one (the little fab, S002); crawl, walk, run (S018); three major iterations (S015, S038); stay cheap and rearrangeable until you know what you are building (tents before the Starfactory, S011); use conventional equipment in an unconventional way, then modify it (S002); ship a new version about every year (S033, S038).
- **When to use:** A first product, a new factory, a new market.
- **How to score:** High if version one is the smallest thing that works and the plan budgets for versions two and three. Low if version one has to be optimized or full scale.
- **Quote:** "In general, with any given technology, you first try to make it work, and then you make it efficient." (S001)
- **Second quote:** "it usually takes three major iterations on a technology for it to be, to be excellent." (S015)
- **Sources:** 8 (S001, S002, S011, S014, S015, S018, S033, S038)

## 12. Aggressive timelines (the 50th percentile deadline) and his known bias
- **What it is:** Set the most aggressive internal deadline you could hit about half the time, because schedules expand to fill whatever time you give them.
- **Parts:** Aim at the 50th percentile (S002); a long schedule expands, which he calls a gas law of schedules (S002, S027); give a best case and a likely case (S007); use a stopgap instead of waiting on a blocked part (stack with a crane before the tower is ready, S009); label external targets as aspirations (S033).
- **His record:** He says "I’m pathologically optimistic on schedule." (S001) Full reusability was "next year" in June 2024 (S011), again in September and October 2025 (S016, S012), and "extremely likely" in September 2026 (S018). Mars was about ten years out in 2016 (S022), a 2022 cargo flight in 2017 (S037), best case five years in 2021 (S006), and about fifty-fifty for late 2026 as of May 2025 (S038).
- **Changed over time:** in 2019 he set hard public dates: "I'd be shocked if it's not next year at the latest" (S005). Newer (2025 to 2026): he calls targets aspirational (S033) and says of a regulatory date "that's not a timeframe we control." (S036)
- **His defense:** "the, the point is not that it was a few years late, but that it happened at all." (S027)
- **When to use:** Internal planning for any team that is moving too slowly.
- **How to score:** Good if the internal deadline is aggressive and the outside promise is hedged. Misfires when customers, lenders, or staff plan their lives around the internal date.
- **Quote:** "I generally actually try to aim for a deadline that I at least think is at the 50th percentile." (S002)
- **Second quote:** "it will expand to fill the available schedule and it’ll take five years" (S002)
- **Sources:** 11 (S001, S002, S006, S007, S009, S011, S015, S027, S029, S033, S036)

## 13. Physical-unit math: think in the limit, to the nearest order of magnitude *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Do the back-of-envelope in real units, push a variable to its extreme to see what still matters, and get the answer right to a factor of ten before acting.
- **Parts:** Back-of-envelope (a car's roof gives about 10 miles of solar range a day, S008); push to the limit (S001, S006, S021); count in units people can feel, like tons (S014); refuse marketing units (two nanometer is a marketing number, S035); size the goal physically (about 300 terawatt-hours of batteries, S024; about 100 Gigafactories, S025).
- **When to use:** Before believing any forecast, market size, or cost claim.
- **How to score:** High if the key numbers survive a factor-of-ten check. Low if a key number is a marketing figure or nobody can say what unit it is in.
- **Quote:** "get the estimate to the nearest order of magnitude, um, you know, closest factor of ten." (S033)
- **Second quote:** "another good physics tool is thinking about things in the limit." (S006)
- **Sources:** 10 (S001, S006, S008, S014, S019, S021, S024, S025, S033, S035)

## 14. Is success in the set of possible outcomes? *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Before starting, ask whether success is even physically and economically possible. If it is, low odds are acceptable; if reviews show success has left the set, act drastically.
- **Parts:** Check feasibility on day one (S015); write down the odds and accept you may lose everything, which shrinks the fear (S022); a small chance beats none (S021); do not attempt what physics rules out (S038); in reviews, watch whether the work converges, and reorganize or replace a team only when success is no longer possible (Starlink in 2018, S002).
- **When to use:** Go or no-go on a big bet, and whether to blow up a struggling team.
- **How to score:** Go if success is in the set and the upside is worth it, even at 10% odds (he gave SpaceX and Tesla under 10%, S022, S028). Drastic action only when progress stops converging.
- **Quote:** "Starship is the first rocket design where success is one of the possible outcomes with full reusability." (S015)
- **Second quote:** "I’ll take drastic action only when I conclude that success is not in a set of possible outcomes." (S002)
- **Sources:** 8 (S002, S003, S010, S015, S021, S022, S028, S038)

## 15. Decompose the impossible *(unnamed; the Colossus build)*
- **What it is:** When told something takes 18 to 24 months and you need it in six, break it into its parts and find a workaround for each one.
- **Parts:** Set the deadline from what you need to compete, not from the supplier quote (S021); list the parts (building, power, cooling, power smoothing, networking for 100,000 GPUs); take what exists now (an existing building, rented generators, about a quarter of US mobile cooling, Megapacks to smooth power swings); run shifts around the clock and do the work yourself; ask every supplier and treat one maybe as a yes (casting, S017); redo it at the next scale with your own transformers (S035).
- **When to use:** A vendor, contractor, or expert says the timeline cannot move.
- **How to score:** High if every part of the impossible timeline has an owner and a workaround. Low if the plan simply accepts the quote.
- **Quote:** "we were told something is impossible, w- but once we broke it down into the constituent elements" (S021)
- **His summary of the job:** "converting things from impossible to late." (S010)
- **Sources:** 6 (S002, S010, S017, S021, S028, S035)

## 16. Same asset, more hours (utilization and full reuse) *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** The expensive thing should be used as many hours as possible and never thrown away. Reuse only counts if it is rapid and complete.
- **Parts:** Compare hours used with hours owned (a car is used about 7% of the 168 hours in a week, S032); design for the real duty cycle (a robotaxi runs 50 to 60 hours a week, so it is built like a commercial truck, S039); reuse that needs months of refurbishing does not count (the Shuttle, S011, S018); full reuse cuts cost per ton about 100 times (S006, S012, S030).
- **When to use:** Capital-heavy assets, equipment, inventory, and any product consumed per use.
- **How to score:** High if the plan raises utilization of its most expensive asset. Low if that asset sits idle or is used up each time.
- **Quote:** "it would be insane to, like, chuck a boat away after every trip." (S011)
- **Second quote:** "it is effectively a fivefold increase in the value of a car overnight." (S032)
- **Sources:** 10 (S006, S011, S012, S018, S025, S029, S030, S032, S037, S039)

## Management and company building

## 17. Time goes to the limiting factor (skip-level reviews and the floor) *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** The leader's calendar follows the bottleneck. He reviews the limiting item weekly or twice weekly with the people doing the work, and spends almost nothing on what is going well.
- **Parts:** Skip-level technical reviews with no advance preparation, plotting each update to see whether the work converges (S002); walk the floor and keep no office (S029); spend Saturdays on the most critical issue (S039); one company per day to cut context switching (S031); schedule your own time, because nobody else knows the priorities (S031); compare every project to the main mission before starting it (the dream house he skipped, S007; planes, S008); 70 to 80% of time on engineering and design, 2 to 3% on press (S022, S023).
- **When to use:** A founder's calendar is full but the main problem is not moving.
- **How to score:** High if the founder's hours map to the current limiting factor. Low if they are spread evenly, or go to meetings, press, and admin.
- **Quote:** "I’m a big believer in skip-level meetings where instead of having the person that reports to me say things, it’s everyone that reports to them saying something in the technical review." (S002)
- **Second quote:** "fear is not the mind killer, context switching is." (S031)
- **Sources:** 11 (S002, S007, S008, S022, S023, S024, S027, S029, S030, S031, S039)

## 18. Requirements with a name, not a department
- **What it is:** Every requirement, constraint, and recurring cost must have a person who owns it and will defend it. A department cannot be questioned; a person can.
- **Parts:** Ask the person, not the department (S014); his example is a requirement traced to an intern who left two years earlier (S014); recurring payments that kept going after the approver left (S012); paid contracts for an empty Twitter building (S019); ask for the real recipient before money goes out (S013, S020).
- **When to use:** Policies, approvals, compliance steps, and subscriptions nobody can explain.
- **How to score:** High if each requirement and recurring cost has a named owner. Low if the only answer is a policy or a department name.
- **Quote:** "whatever requirement or constraint you have, it must come with a name, not a department." (S014)
- **Sources:** 5 (S012, S013, S014, S019, S020)

## 19. Everyone is chief engineer (the leader must know the product)
- **What it is:** People need to see the whole system so they can tell when their local optimization hurts the whole, and the person in charge must understand the product in detail.
- **Parts:** Everyone understands the system at a high level (the booster team tuned engine mass and landed with a ton of unused fuel, S014); he is chief engineer himself, first because he could not hire one (S006, S012, S021, S037); a CEO who never visits the factory is the warning sign (his Boeing example, S015); no ivory-tower management (S030).
- **When to use:** Hiring a CEO or operator, or when teams hit their own targets while the product gets worse.
- **How to score:** High if decision makers can explain how the product works and is made. Low if the company is run by people who never touch it.
- **Quote:** "So everyone is chief engineer means that people need to understand the, the system at a high level" (S014)
- **Second quote:** "if you're the cavalry captain, you should know how to ride a horse." (S015)
- **Sources:** 7 (S006, S012, S014, S015, S021, S030, S037)

## 20. Hire for evidence of exceptional ability *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Ask for specific evidence of exceptional work, weigh the live conversation over the resume, and hire traits first (talent, drive, trustworthiness, goodness of heart); domain knowledge can be added.
- **Parts:** Bullet points of evidence, where three strong items beat one (S002); no magic from a big-company brand (S002); recruit the best actively, like a sports team (S017); pay well above average (S020); the company that attracts the most driven people wins (S032); hire when you have tried a skill and failed at it (Franz von Holzhausen for design, S008); expect leadership to turn over as the company changes size (S002).
- **When to use:** Any key hire, and especially a hire justified by a famous former employer.
- **How to score:** High if the case for the hire is specific achievements tested in conversation. Low if it rests on credentials or brand.
- **Quote:** "Generally, the things I ask for are bullet points for evidence of exceptional ability." (S002)
- **His correction:** "And I think goodness of heart is important. I underweighted that at one point." (S002)
- **Sources:** 6 (S002, S008, S017, S020, S023, S032)

## 21. Incentives explain behavior *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** To predict what a person or organization will do, look at what it is paid and punished for. Asymmetric punishment kills change.
- **Parts:** Ask what each side is rewarded for (fund managers judged quarterly push short-term results, S013); regulators are punished for approving and not for blocking (S008); the Shuttle team was punished hard for a failed change and rewarded little for a good one, so nobody fixed known issues (S009); a payment per case changes how cases get counted (S007).
- **When to use:** Predicting a partner, regulator, investor, or your own team; designing comp and review systems.
- **How to score:** High if the plan pays people for the behavior it needs. Low if it relies on goodwill against their incentives.
- **Quote:** "but when you, when you understand the incentives, then, then you understand the behavior." (S012)
- **Sources:** 5 (S007, S008, S009, S012, S013)

## 22. Garbage collection for rules *(his phrase)*
- **What it is:** Rules pile up like code that is never removed, until nothing can move. Removing a rule should be easier than adding one, and removals should be reversible.
- **Parts:** Add a sunset to rules (S006); make removal easier than addition (S006, S028); put a rule back if people want it (S015); keep enough referees to keep the game safe, not so many you cannot kick the ball (S026); approval should never be slower than building (S015).
- **When to use:** He applies it mostly to government. For a company, the same test fits internal policy, approval chains, and sign-offs (inferred from S014, S015).
- **How to score:** High if the plan includes a way to retire old rules. Low if each new problem gets a new rule and nothing is ever removed.
- **Quote:** "it's easy to add rules, but we don't actually have a process for getting rid of them. That's the issue. There's no garbage collection for rules." (S015)
- **Sources:** 5 (S006, S015, S026, S028, S030)

## 23. Close the loop on reality *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Build feedback that tells you fast when you are wrong: negative feedback over praise, outside graders over self-grading, and every required user action treated as an error.
- **Parts:** Ask friends what they do not like (S003); treat all user input as error and shrink it (S005, S008); do not grade your own homework (S018); publish the analysis and invite critique (S032); an ego bigger than ability breaks the feedback loop (S021); reality is the judge (S001).
- **When to use:** Product reviews, launch decisions, and any plan whose evidence comes from the team or its fans.
- **How to score:** High if there is a fast channel for bad news. Low if all feedback is praise or comes from people grading their own work.
- **Quote:** "you wanna just close the loop on reality hard." (S021)
- **Second quote:** "Tell me what you don't like." (S003)
- **Sources:** 7 (S001, S003, S005, S008, S018, S021, S032)

## 24. Control and cash for a long horizon *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Hold enough cash to survive irrational times and enough control to protect a horizon longer than investors' feedback loop.
- **Parts:** Keep reserves and get to positive cash flow fast in a downturn (S017); raise while the window is open (PayPal in March 2000, S017); stay private when the horizon is longer than the public market's, which he puts at a year to four years or even quarterly (S008, S027); go public only with enough voting control to steer (S013, S026), which he set at about 25%, enough to steer and little enough to be fired (S019); avoid a capital-intensive first company (S023).
- **When to use:** Fundraising, going public, taking board seats, and spending in a downturn.
- **How to score:** High if runway survives a bad year and control matches the horizon. Low if the plan needs every month to go right or hands control to investors with a shorter horizon.
- **Quote:** "make sure you're not running things too close to the edge from a capital standpoint, that you've got some capital reserves to, uh, last through, uh, irrational times." (S017)
- **Second quote:** "sufficient voting control such that, um, I cannot be ousted by activist investors is what matters to me" (S026)
- **Sources:** 8 (S007, S008, S013, S017, S019, S023, S026, S027)

## 25. Utility delta times people (what to work on) *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Size a project by how much better it is than the current best, times how many people it reaches. A big gain for a few and a small gain for many can both be worth it.
- **Parts:** Improvement over the state of the art times people affected (S022); the counterfactual: how many years it speeds up something that would happen anyway (S025, S028); output worth more than input (S020); money follows usefulness rather than being chased (S020).
- **When to use:** Choosing between projects, products, or markets.
- **How to score:** High if the plan can state its improvement and its reach. Low if the main case is money or status.
- **Quote:** "whatever this thing is that you're trying to create, what would, what would be the, um, utility delta compared to the current state-of-the-art times how many people it would affect?" (S022)
- **Second quote:** "just be focused on having the output be worth more than the input." (S020)
- **Sources:** 5 (S020, S021, S022, S025, S028)
