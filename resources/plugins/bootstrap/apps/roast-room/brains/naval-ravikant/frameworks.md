# Frameworks: Naval Ravikant

25 frameworks, roughly most-used first, built from the eight extract batches over 35 sources (2009 to 2026). "Sources" counts distinct sources where the idea is used (ids listed). Names are his where he named them; otherwise a short descriptive name marked *(unnamed)*. The How-to-score lines are scoring guidance for the council built from his stated thresholds, not quotes. The seat's question (leverage and equity you own versus rented time) leans hardest on 1, 2, 8, and 9.

---

## 1. Three kinds of leverage: labor, capital, code and media
- **What it is:** Leverage multiplies the output of your judgment. Labor (people working for you) is the oldest and worst form: messy, fought over, and overvalued by society. Capital ruled the last century and needs someone to fund you. Code and media cost nothing to copy, need nobody's permission, and are where new fortunes get made. In 2025 he adds AI, agents, robots, and supply chains to the list (S034).
- **Parts:** Name the form of leverage the plan uses; prefer permissionless (code, writing, podcasts, video) over permissioned (labor, capital); use the minimum labor needed to unlock the other forms; a tech company combines all three. Headcount is a weak signal of impact.
- **When to use:** Any plan that grows by adding hours or people; a service business versus a product; whether to hire or build.
- **How to score:** 5 if output scales through code or media at near-zero marginal cost and the founder's hours are not the bottleneck; 3 if capital or a small team does the multiplying; 1 if revenue rises only with more hours or more people. In S018 he puts numbers on how cheap leverage got: setup that cost $1M to $2M now costs $10K.
- **Quote:** "Leverage comes in labor, comes in capital, or it can come through code or media." (S001) Also: "Probably the most interesting thing to keep in mind about the new forms of leverage is they are permissionless." (S001)
- **Sources:** 8 (S001, S017, S018, S030, S031, S032, S033, S034)

## 2. Judgment in an age of infinite leverage
- **What it is:** When capital, code, media, and teams multiply every decision, being right a little more often is worth far more than working more. Judgment is built by doing, reflecting, and generalizing, until it becomes taste you cannot articulate.
- **Parts:** Decision quality over effort; a clear, rested mind; the judgment ladder (reason through it, then the subconscious joins, then the body, then taste) (S032, S029); prepare before the decision arrives (S025); pay for the best judgment, human or model (S035). Hustle first to get leverage, then slow down because judgment now matters (S001).
- **When to use:** Whether to add effort or think harder; hiring a leader; any high-stakes call that capital or code will multiply.
- **How to score:** His example in S032: two CEO candidates, one right 80 percent of the time and one 85 percent, at a company worth trillions, are billions of dollars apart. Score 5 if the founder slowed down to think on a high-leverage call; 1 if they are grinding hours on a decision their leverage will amplify. In S025 he says judgment takes five to 15 years to verify in early-stage investing, so self-reported judgment counts for little.
- **Quote:** "In an age of infinite leverage, judgment becomes the most important skill." (S001) Also: "Judgment is the preparation you do before a deal arrives so that your subconscious can process it quickly." (S025)
- **Sources:** 8 (S001, S017, S025, S029, S031, S032, S033, S035)

## 3. Power law and asymmetric bets
- **What it is:** Returns are nonlinear. The number one outcome outweighs everything else combined, so you only need to be right once. Take bets where you can lose 1x and make 10,000x, see many, pick a few, and double down on the winners.
- **Parts:** Capped downside, open upside; a wide funnel (10,000 companies seen, 500 picked, 5 doubled down on) (S031); grand slams over many small wins (S025); spend time and capital only where 100x to 1,000x is possible (S011); you are judged by your best work, not your average (S033).
- **When to use:** A safe small win versus a long shot; how to spread effort across projects; whether a product line can ever be the big one.
- **How to score:** 5 if loss is capped and upside is 100x or more; 3 if upside is around 10x; 1 if upside is linear (2x or 3x) or downside is uncapped. In S025 he prefers nine zeros and one 1,000x over a portfolio of 2x and 3x outcomes.
- **Quote:** "on the downside you can only lose 1 X but on the upside you can make 10,000 X so I tried to rig the game" (S031) Also: "it only makes sense to do that for something that has that opportunity to be 100x or 1,000x on your time or on your capital." (S011)
- **Sources:** 8 (S001, S011, S024, S025, S030, S031, S033, S034)

## 4. Curate people (geniuses only)
- **What it is:** The early people are the company's DNA, so the founder recruits personally, holds a bar with no compromise, and fires to keep it. Buffett's test (intelligence, energy, integrity) plus low ego and self-motivation.
- **Parts:** The three-part test, with integrity read from actions and how someone treats third parties (S031, S025); the random-teammate test (a candidate may interview anyone for 30 minutes and should walk if not impressed) (S007); collect geniuses even without a slot; first engineers are late founders with founder equity (S019); pay twice, give a lot of stock, expect ten times the output (S034); hire slowly, fire fast.
- **When to use:** Any hire, cofounder, partner, or investor; outsourcing recruiting; a team that has stopped impressing new candidates.
- **How to score:** 5 if the person clears all three legs and the founder recruited them directly; 1 if hired to fill a slot, recruited by an agency, or high-ego. Guidance from S007: you are lucky to hire one genius a month, which caps a company near 30 to 50 people, and one manager handles 30 or 40 low-ego people versus five high-ego ones.
- **Quote:** "I would summarize it in two words: Curate people." (S007) Also: "In terms of picking people to work with, pick ones that have high intelligence, high energy, and high integrity, I find that’s the three-part checklist that you cannot compromise on." (S001)
- **Sources:** 8 (S001, S007, S011, S019, S025, S031, S032, S034)

## 5. Network effects, zero marginal cost, scale economies *(unnamed checklist)*
- **What it is:** The business-model version of leverage. The three travel together, and in technology they produce winner-take-all markets: one aggregator per category, a long tail of individuals, and a hollowed-out middle. There is no demand for average, but the set of things you can be best at is infinite.
- **Parts:** Do customers add value to each other; does the next unit cost nothing; does unit cost fall with volume; are you number one in the category, or in a niche you keep redefining until you are.
- **When to use:** Choosing a model or market; entering a crowded category; a mid-sized player that relies on geography or regulation.
- **How to score:** 5 if all three are present and the company can be first in its category or niche; 3 if one or two; 1 if it is a mid-sized player with linear costs in a market heading to one winner. Being number two in a network-effects market scores low.
- **Quote:** "it’s a really good idea to pick a model where you can benefit from network effects, low marginal costs, and scale economies; and these tend to go together." (S001) Also: "It destroys the mediocre middle, which was relying on geography or you know regulations to survive." (S032)
- **Sources:** 7 (S001, S002, S006, S017, S018, S032, S035)

## 6. Minimum-sized group (scale is your enemy) *(his phrase in S011)*
- **What it is:** Use the smallest group of high-trust, highly competent people that can do the job. Large groups seek consensus rather than truth and never admit failure. Hierarchy is a cost of size, not a virtue.
- **Parts:** A fully interconnected graph with one light hub who holds the whole picture (S009); no Slack, one-to-one communication, maker's time; one person holds the whole product in their head (S007, S029); add structure only when size forces it; unscale as founders like Jobs, Musk, and Bezos did.
- **When to use:** Growing headcount, adding managers, process, or internal tools.
- **How to score:** 5 if output grows without new layers and every person can navigate the flat graph; 1 if structure, managers, or tools are added ahead of size. His own company in 2025 is under 20 people, mostly in person (S034).
- **Quote:** "So you want to be in the minimum-sized group to do the scope of the job that you set out to do." (S011) Also: "Scale is your enemy." (S007)
- **Sources:** 7 (S007, S009, S011, S013, S017, S032, S034)

## 7. AI as leverage: the smartest model, waste tokens, humans as verifiers *(2026 cluster)*
- **What it is:** His 2026 operating stance on AI. Always use the most intelligent model because errors are invisible and every answer gets multiplied; judge only your time and the final output, not token cost; do not learn tricks, because the model improves faster than you do; people move from generating to verifying and standing behind the work.
- **Parts:** Smartest model always (errors compound across agent loops, S035); throw several models at a solved, verifiable problem and spend more tokens on review before shipping; work closely with the model on unsolved frontier problems; vibe code to prototype, hire real engineers and rewrite to scale (S006); automate anything repetitive; use AI for retrieval, not the judgment call at the edge (S032); write human-facing output yourself (S035).
- **When to use:** Tooling and model spend; whether a task should still be done by a person; how to staff around agents.
- **How to score:** 5 if the founder buys the best intelligence available and spends their own time only on taste, verification, and judgment; 1 if they save on model cost while spending their own hours, or hand the final judgment call to a model. Compare model cost to a human, not to zero (S012).
- **Quote:** "Don’t look at the tokens either as inputs or outputs. Just look at your time, and look at the final output." (S012) Also: "Humans are becoming verifiers." (S003)
- **View change:** In 2019 he said "we're nowhere near close to general ai not in our lifetimes" (S017). On 2026-07-02 he says full replacement is something "which I don't see around the corner, but I'm more open-minded about it now" (S035). The newer view governs.
- **Sources:** 7 (S002, S003, S006, S008, S012, S032, S035)

## 8. Own equity, do not rent your time (hierarchy of capital)
- **What it is:** You cannot get rich when you stop earning as you sleep. Salary is paid first and capped, debt gets a fixed coupon, and equity takes the risk for nearly unlimited upside. Taking accountability is an equity position in your own work.
- **Parts:** Own a piece as founder, early employee, investor, or brand; align partners through shared equity rather than terms (S032); in pre-traction companies close the equity gap and treat the first two engineers as late founders (S019); before accepting equity as payment, discount it to present value (S001).
- **When to use:** Fee versus salary versus revenue share versus ownership; pricing a service business; paying early hires and partners.
- **How to score:** 5 if the founder owns an asset that earns without them (product, IP, company stake, brand); 3 if income mixes fees with equity; 1 if income stops when they stop working. NPV guidance from S001: a 0.1 percent stake in a company someone calls a billion-dollar business, last valued at $10 million, is worth about $10,000 today.
- **Quote:** "You’re not going to get rich renting out your time." (S024) Also: "need to have equity either as an owner an investor shareholder or a brand that you're building that accrues to you to gain your financial freedom" (S017)
- **Sources:** 6 (S001, S017, S019, S024, S030, S032)

## 9. Specific knowledge, accountability, leverage, judgment (productize yourself)
- **What it is:** The four ingredients of wealth. Specific knowledge is what only you do well; it cannot be trained, or you could be replaced. Accountability puts your name on the outcome. Leverage scales it, and judgment steers it. Productize yourself is his two-word summary.
- **Parts:** Specific knowledge feels like play to you and work to others, often sits at the edge of technology, art, or communication, and is usually a skill stack of two or three things (S005, S034); you only find it by acting in hard situations (S029); timely knowledge expires and timeless knowledge (sales, persuasion) must be stacked (S001); put your name on it; pick a leverage form.
- **When to use:** Founder-market fit; a creator or consultant deciding whether to productize; personal brand decisions.
- **How to score:** Ask why this founder, at scale. 5 if they have a rare skill stack, sell under their own name, and scale it with code or media; 3 if two of the three; 1 if the work is trainable and anyone could be hired to do it.
- **Quote:** "Productize has specific knowledge and leverage. Yourself has uniqueness and accountability." (S001) Also: "You are not going to know your own specific knowledge until you act and until you act in a variety of difficult situations." (S029)
- **Sources:** 6 (S001, S005, S017, S029, S030, S034)

## 10. Long-term games with long-term people
- **What it is:** Every return in money, relationships, and learning comes from compound interest, and trust is what lets it compound. Repeated games reward cooperation, so pick an industry and people that will be around for decades.
- **Parts:** Stay in the industry where your network lives (each hop starts trust over); pick partners who signal staying power and show ethics in action; when someone is taking advantage, turn the short game into a long one (references, future work); bake the pie rather than cut it; give credit away.
- **When to use:** Partnerships, deal terms, pivots into a new industry, whether to squeeze a counterparty.
- **How to score:** 5 if the move builds trust with people the founder will deal with for years; 1 if it wins this deal at the cost of reputation or resets the network. In S031 he says unfair deals unwind into short-term relationships with no compounding. His compounding numbers: 20 percent a year for 30 years (S004).
- **Quote:** "Essentially, all the benefits in life come from compound interests." (S004) Also: "If someone is taking advantage of you in a negotiation, your best option is to turn it from a short-term game into a long-term game." (S001)
- **Sources:** 6 (S001, S004, S016, S031, S032, S034)

## 11. Wealth, money, and status
- **What it is:** Wealth is assets that earn while you sleep; money is how wealth moves; status is rank. Wealth creation is positive sum; status is zero sum and always takes combat. From 2025 he also uses David Deutsch's definition, wealth as the set of physical transformations you can bring about, which makes knowledge the bigger part (S028, S032).
- **Parts:** Ask whether the plan builds assets and knowledge or buys rank; pursue wealth indirectly through value; overt status chasing signals low status (S029); get rich first, fame after.
- **When to use:** Spend on image (office, events, cars) versus assets; fame-first content versus productized value; a founder who wants recognition.
- **How to score:** 5 if the move adds an asset that earns or knowledge the company owns; 1 if it buys rank, attention, or a status signal with no asset behind it.
- **Quote:** "Wealth is assets that earn while you sleep; it’s the factory of robots cranking out things." (S001) Also: "wealth creation is positive but status is limited." (S030)
- **Sources:** 6 (S001, S017, S028, S029, S030, S032)

## 12. Inputs versus outputs
- **What it is:** Pick work where hours in do not map to results out. In idea domains output differs by 100x to 1,000x, and choosing the right problem is an infinite difference. Pay and measure outputs, not hours.
- **Parts:** Is the founder's best hour worth 100 of their average; are people paid on results; pay twice and expect ten times the output (S034).
- **When to use:** Compensation design, hiring, and the founder's own week.
- **How to score:** 5 if pay and progress are tied to outputs that leverage multiplies; 1 if work is billed or judged by hours or days.
- **Quote:** "You really just want a job, or a career, or a profession where your inputs don’t match your outputs." (S001) Also: "if you choose the right thing to work on versus the wrong thing to work on, that’s an infinity difference." (S012)
- **Sources:** 6 (S001, S003, S012, S017, S018, S034)

## 13. Play that looks like work (pursue it indirectly)
- **What it is:** Find what feels like play to you and looks like work to others. Someone who loves the work beats someone who does not, so money arrives as a byproduct of building something of value with leverage, accountability, and specific knowledge. No one can compete with you on being you.
- **Parts:** Does the founder enjoy it a lot; would they do it with no audience (Rogan's early streams, S029); is the work step A only to reach B (S030); is it authentic.
- **When to use:** Picking a business or marketing channel; starting content only to market something.
- **How to score:** 5 if the founder would do it unpaid and already does it daily; 1 if it was chosen only for the money or as a detour to something else. Burnout counts as evidence the fit is wrong (S034).
- **Quote:** "find what feels like play to you but looks like work to others." (S030) Also: "if you’re building something of value and you’re using leverage and you’re taking accountability and you’re applying your specific knowledge, you’re going to make money as a byproduct." (S029)
- **Sources:** 6 (S001, S017, S029, S030, S032, S034)

## 14. Iterations, not hours (life is lived in the arena)
- **What it is:** Mastery comes from learning loops, not time spent: do it, reflect honestly on the outcome, change it, try again. His replacement for 10,000 hours. Principles only make sense once you have specifics to attach them to.
- **Parts:** Count iterations; test against markets, nature, or physics; bail out fast from losers; learn by doing first, then generalize.
- **When to use:** A founder who is planning instead of shipping; a slow learning cycle; how to judge a team's speed.
- **How to score:** 5 if the team runs many real tests a month against paying customers; 1 if it is polishing a plan or a deck.
- **Quote:** "It’s not time spent. It’s learning loops." (S029) Also: "It’s the number of iterations that drives the learning curve." (S001)
- **Sources:** 6 (S001, S002, S007, S029, S030, S032)

## 15. Where the moat moved *(unnamed; 2025 to 2026)*
- **What it is:** Pure software is now easy to build, so it is no longer a moat or a venture bet. Value moves to hardware, network effects, design, and the frontier AI labs, while China owns subsidized hardware scale.
- **Parts:** Can an agent build this from a spec; does the business have hardware, network effects, or design that resists copying; is it in a saturated SaaS category (S034).
- **When to use:** Pricing or funding a software product; deciding whether to build software as the core asset.
- **How to score:** 5 if the asset is hard to copy even with agents (network, hardware, design, proprietary data); 1 if it is a feature an agent could reproduce from a description.
- **Quote:** "pure software is uninvestable. I would just full stop right there." (S006) Also: "I think design is always a moat." (S034) And: "hardware is the moat that buys you time to build a software castle" (S035)
- **View change:** In 2009 he called web leverage "obscene" because products had no marginal cost (S018), and in 2019 coding was the way to command "the robot armies" (S001). By 2026-04-28 pure software is uninvestable (S006). The newer view governs; he still says engineers are the most leveraged people (S032).
- **Sources:** 6 (S003, S006, S009, S012, S034, S035)

## 16. Explore, then exploit (life is a search function)
- **What it is:** Early on, say yes widely to search for the right work, people, and place. Once you find the fit, switch to exploitation: say no by default and compound. You cannot skip either phase.
- **Parts:** Who, what, and where decide most outcomes (S030); spend real time on them; then move all your chips in.
- **When to use:** A founder deciding whether to add a second business; whether to keep testing or commit.
- **How to score:** Early-stage founder sampling many things scores fine; a founder with traction who keeps opening new fronts scores low. The two failure modes he names are committing too early and never committing (S032).
- **Quote:** "So there's an exploration phase and there's the exploitation phase." (S032) Also: "Life is a search function." (S034)
- **Sources:** 5 (S016, S030, S031, S032, S034)

## 17. Learn to sell, learn to build
- **What it is:** Two broad skills. Building covers design, development, manufacturing, and operations; selling covers marketing, recruiting, fundraising, and PR. A founding pair of one builder and one seller is the classic pattern, and one person who does both is unstoppable.
- **Parts:** Start by building to stand out; shift weight to selling later because sales scale better; a seller must know the product well enough not to be fooled; everyone on a founding team should be highly technical or great at selling (S025).
- **When to use:** Founding team gaps; a technical founder avoiding sales; early hires (he wants more technical builders than marketers at the start, S032).
- **How to score:** 5 if the team has a world-class builder and a world-class seller (or one person who is both); 1 if it has neither.
- **Quote:** "So, I think the real giants in any field are the people who can both build and sell." (S001) Also: "The ultimate is when one individual can do both. That’s when you get true superpowers." (S005)
- **Sources:** 5 (S001, S005, S007, S025, S032)

## 18. Feedback from reality
- **What it is:** Only nature, physics, and free markets give feedback you cannot fool. Praise, awards, critics, and groups give fake feedback. Structures that cannot lose money cannot learn.
- **Parts:** Is the test against customers paying or physics working; is the scorecard written by people with nothing at stake; does the founder optimize for awards or press.
- **When to use:** Validating demand; reading a board's or community's approval; choosing between a for-profit and a non-profit structure.
- **How to score:** 5 if the evidence is money paid or a system that works; 1 if it is applause, awards, or a group's own report.
- **Quote:** "Physics is harsh: either your product worked, or it didn’t. Free markets are harsh: either people buy it, or they don’t." (S029) Also: "If you want to change the world to a better place, the best way to do it is a for-profit because for-profits have to take feedback from reality." (S013)
- **Sources:** 5 (S010, S013, S029, S033, S034)

## 19. No by default *(unnamed decision heuristics)*
- **What it is:** His stack for choices. If you cannot decide, the answer is no. A new opportunity that changes your starting point defaults to no. If two paths look equal, take the one that hurts more in the short term. Prefer the choice that leaves you calmer in the long run. If it is not a hell yes, it is a no.
- **Parts:** Sleep on hard calls and wait for the gut answer with conviction (S030); match deciding time to commitment length; check any commitment again after 48 hours (S031); a 24-hour cooling-off period on deals (S025).
- **When to use:** New offers, partnerships, side projects, speaking slots, and any yes that fills the future calendar.
- **How to score:** 5 if the founder is excited now and would take it today; 1 if they are on the fence or saying yes to avoid disappointing someone.
- **Quote:** "if you can't decide, the answer is no." (S030) Also: "easy choices hard life hard choices easy life" (S031)
- **Sources:** 4 (S016, S025, S030, S031)

## 20. Optionality and the walk-away deal
- **What it is:** A contract is a stag hunt: both sides give up options for a bigger shared prize, so only sign if it is among your best paths. Compromise is the enemy of a great business. Keep your time and optionality free so you can walk.
- **Parts:** Raise six to 12 months before you need cash, and only when your own excitement crosses a threshold (S011); judge money by its terms, not its source (S022); prefer an aligned partnership to one where you hold the advantage (S001); fight only if letting it go would cost a year of sleep, otherwise walk (S011).
- **When to use:** Term sheets, partnerships, exclusivity, board seats, preferred stock, vetoes, and disputes.
- **How to score:** 5 if the founder has a real alternative and the deal keeps their options; 1 if they are signing under time pressure into terms they cannot unwind.
- **Quote:** "A contract is when two parties agree to constrain their future outcome in exchange for working together." (S011) Also: "I definitely lean on the side of walking away from suboptimal deals rather than doing something just to get it done." (S011)
- **Sources:** 4 (S001, S011, S022, S032)

## 21. Capital, judgment, dealflow (non-consensus and right)
- **What it is:** His angel-investing model. You need capital, judgment, and dealflow, and access (a brand founders trust) gates the best deals. The money is made when you are right and the room disagrees. The best deals are weird, and each is allowed one fatal flaw that lets conventional investors pass.
- **Parts:** Dealflow is not access; being cut out of a round signals a winner; start with your own network; break the false rules and keep the ones that work (S025); first-time founders take market risk, repeat founders take execution risk.
- **When to use:** Angel bets, choosing which opportunities to back, judging a weird idea the room dislikes.
- **How to score:** Guidance from S024: if you do more than one in ten deals you look at, you are too optimistic. Score 5 for a non-consensus bet with one flaw and a great founder; 1 for a consensus bet in a hot market found through conferences and press.
- **Quote:** "The three things it takes to get into investing are capital, judgment and dealflow." (S024) Also: "The best deals are weird. They always have something broken, strange or different about them." (S025)
- **Sources:** 4 (S024, S025, S020, S031)

## 22. How funding markets behave (reverse development, herds, unbundled VC)
- **What it is:** Private investors herd rationally, because a deal financed now is likely financed later (a Keynesian beauty contest) (S020). In any region, money for the safest companies arrives first and seed money last, so frontier founders bootstrap to real returns or move the headquarters (S021). VC was a bundle of advice, control, and money, and the bundle is breaking apart (S022).
- **Parts:** Downstream financing risk is the main risk an early investor carries (S021); judge money by terms; do not join a seed program for the money.
- **When to use:** Where to base a company; whether to raise; which investor type to take.
- **How to score:** 5 if the founder can fund growth from revenue or from a hub with follow-on capital; 1 if the plan needs several future rounds in a market without them.
- **Quote:** "In any given geographic region, the first companies that get funded are the ones that least need funding." (S021) Also: "It is rational for private investors to move in herds." (S020)
- **View change:** In 2010 to 2019 he built funding infrastructure (AngelList, Spearhead) (S021, S024). In 2025 he says "I won’t take money from any VC." (S007), and in 2026 he funds his own company and says most great companies were self-financed or broke out before needing VC (S032). The newer view governs for his own companies.
- **Sources:** 4 (S020, S021, S022, S024)

## 23. Aspirational hourly rate
- **What it is:** Pick an hourly rate that feels absurdly high (his was $5,000 an hour before he had money; S017 says he moved from $500 to $5,000) and never spend time below it. Outsource or drop any task that costs less.
- **Parts:** Set the rate; hire out anything cheaper; skip non-transactional meetings and business travel (S017); overpay for the best help (S030).
- **When to use:** The founder's calendar; doing work a hire could do; meetings, travel, and small savings.
- **How to score:** 5 if the founder spends their week on work worth the rate; 1 if they do tasks they could buy for a tenth of it, or penny-pinch while building wealth.
- **Quote:** "Set a very high aspirational hourly rate for yourself, and stick to it. It should seem and feel absurdly high. If it doesn’t, it’s not high enough." (S001)
- **Sources:** 3 (S001, S017, S030)

## 24. Avoid ruin (Kelly, ergodicity, barbell)
- **What it is:** Even with an edge, never bet everything on one hand, because ruin is absorbing: the average across many people is not what one person gets repeating the bet. The common ruin in business is cutting corners or breaking the law. Be skeptical of each specific bet and optimistic in general, then go all in when the right one appears.
- **Parts:** No all-in bets of capital; no reputation risk; do not invest money you are not OK losing (S025); iterate and cut losses on specific bets (S030).
- **When to use:** Personal guarantees, big debt, betting the company, shortcuts on law or ethics.
- **How to score:** 5 if the worst case is survivable; 1 if one bad outcome wipes out the founder, the company, or their name.
- **Quote:** "The simple concept is: Don’t risk everything. Stay out of jail. Don’t bet everything on one big gamble." (S001) Also: "Every specific opportunity is probably a fail. But you want to be optimistic in the general." (S030)
- **Sources:** 3 (S001, S025, S030)

## 25. Good products are hard to vary
- **What it is:** From David Deutsch's test for good explanations. A design is right when the parts constrain each other so no detail can change without breaking it. Constraints create the capability, and a hard-to-vary product reaches markets its makers never planned for (iPhone over BlackBerry in enterprise).
- **Parts:** Remove until nothing is left to remove; choices left to the user are an abdication (S007); one opinionated holder of the vision.
- **When to use:** Product scope, feature creep, multiple versions or tiers.
- **How to score:** 5 if every feature is load-bearing and rivals cannot materially vary it; 1 if parts could be swapped freely. Selling several versions needs a very good reason (S032).
- **Quote:** "good products are hard to vary." (S032) Also: "So it was the constraints that created the capability above." (S032)
- **Sources:** 3 (S028, S029, S032)
