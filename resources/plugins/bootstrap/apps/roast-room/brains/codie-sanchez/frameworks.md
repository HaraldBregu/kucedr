# Frameworks: Codie Sanchez

25 frameworks built from her interviews, her own channel, her two books as she teaches them, and her signed newsletter pieces (2017 to 2026), roughly most-used first. "Sources" counts distinct sources where the idea is used (ids listed). Names are hers where she named them; otherwise a short descriptive name marked *(unnamed)*. The How-to-score lines are scoring guidance for the council built from her stated thresholds, not quotes. Contrarian Thinking newsletters signed by her team (S032 to S034, S036 to S039, S042, S043) are company voice: they are cited only as support and never quoted as her words.

---

## 1. Buy the odds: startups versus acquisitions *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Her core answer to build or buy. A startup is a bet against a very high failure rate and years of little pay; a business that has already survived has beaten that curve, so buying it moves you to a better table.
- **Parts:** Compare failure rates (startups fail most of the time; bought businesses far less often); compare the founder's first years of pay (startups cost money and pay the founder little for years); ask whether the business already has product-market fit and positive cash flow; if it does, much of the risk is already gone.
- **When to use:** Any time a founder proposes building something from zero to make money, or weighs a new product line against acquiring one.
- **How to score:** 5 if the alternative to building is a profitable business with years of history at a sane price; 3 if buying is possible but costly or the targets are thin; 1 if nothing buyable does the job and the founder truly needs to invent. Her numbers move around (90% of startups fail in S002, S011, S016; 13% a year for SBA-backed purchases in S002 and S016; 80% of acquisitions survive year one in S020; 5% to 25% in S011), so treat them as rhetorical anchors, not one data set.
- **Quote:** "Buy all day long. 90% of businesses fail. Businesses you buy have a 13% failure rate on average according to the SBA." (S016)
- **Sources:** 12 (S002, S006, S007, S010, S011, S012, S014, S016, S020, S021, S022, S035)

## 2. Seller financing pitch: time, money, taxes
- **What it is:** Let the retiring owner act as the bank and get paid from the business's future profits. She sells it to the owner as a better deal than a bank sale, not as a favor.
- **Parts:** Earn trust first; show the bank values the business below the owner's ask; offer a higher total price paid over time with interest going to the owner; point out a faster close and taxes spread over years. Worked example: owner wants $1M, bank says $750K at 8%, buyer offers $1.15M over time (S020, S021). She says pitching it as a win-win roughly doubled how often owners accepted (S014).
- **When to use:** The buyer is short on cash, the seller is older and tired, the business is simple, and there is no obvious successor.
- **How to score:** High if the seller fits the avatar (retiring, few buyers, no successor) and the business's cash flow covers the note with room to spare; low if the seller is young, has many offers, or the deal only works with bank debt the business strains to carry. She says about 60% of small businesses sell with some seller financing (S008, S014, S018, S020), though in S021 she gives 60% for SBA loans instead.
- **Quote:** "Seller financing is just removing the bank." (S020)
- **Sources:** 13 (S001, S007, S008, S011, S013, S014, S018, S020, S021, S022, S023, S025, S026)

## 3. Deal box and Deal Clarity
- **What it is:** A written filter for what you will buy before you look at anything, like Zillow filters for a house. She refuses to name the "best sector" because the right business depends on the buyer.
- **Parts:** Deal Clarity is three circles: profit (makes you enough and is feasible), obsession (what you could do continuously), and probability (where your skills give the best odds) (S012). The worksheet asks what you want, how much you need to earn, how much money and risk you will put in, where, and what you will not do (S001, S007). The deal box then sets price range, income needed, financing, location, industry, owner-operator or owner-investor, must-haves, and can't-haves (S020). Earlier version: the Unfair Advantage Venn of skills, network, and obsession (S001).
- **When to use:** Before any search, and whenever a founder falls for a shiny deal that does not match what they need.
- **How to score:** Pass a target that fails the box. Score the founder high if they can state their box in a few lines (income needed, size, location, sector, financing); low if they are chasing whatever is in front of them.
- **Quote:** "You will never find a deal if you do not get specific about what you want." (S020)
- **Sources:** 10 (S001, S005, S007, S008, S012, S018, S020, S021, S022, S023)

## 4. Three currencies to buy: money, expertise, sweat (the get rich tripod)
- **What it is:** You can buy into a business with cash, with expertise, or with time. You only need one, so "I have no money" is not a reason to skip ownership.
- **Parts:** Money is the highest leverage, so use it first if you have it (S008). Expertise beats sweat because it carries leverage (S022). Expertise to equity: offer to raise revenue, cut costs, or remove the owner's pain in exchange for a share (S008, S018). Equity for growth: offer to double profit for 15% to 20%, or take nothing if you fail (S001). Her proof is Strike Fire Productions, 49% for $10K plus running the sales the owner hated (S014, S020, S021, S022). For a failing business, buy the customers on a revenue share instead of the business (S006).
- **When to use:** A founder with skills but little capital, or one who could buy part of a business instead of starting a rival.
- **How to score:** High if the buyer brings a skill the owner lacks and can name the revenue or cost they will move; low if they bring only enthusiasm. She says broke and ignorant does not qualify (S021).
- **Quote:** "you could buy it with your sweat and your time so there's three ways to buy a business" (S001)
- **Sources:** 10 (S001, S006, S008, S010, S014, S018, S020, S021, S022, S023)

## 5. Downside first: go to zero and the one thing that could kill you
- **What it is:** Before upside, model how the deal dies, and size every deal so the worst case cannot take you out.
- **Parts:** Never do a bad first deal (S001); size the first deal so a loss is annoying, not fatal (S005, S007, S011); stress test losing 20%, 50%, and 75% of revenue or profit and pass if the debt is no longer covered; ask whether the business could go to zero and build protection such as escrow holdbacks; find the one thing that could kill it (equipment is common, so get a third-party valuation); cap any deal at 20% of net worth, which she later tightened to about 5%; keep about 12 months of emergency cash (S027). She leans on Buffett's never-lose-money rule (S024, S026).
- **When to use:** Every deal and every big bet, especially a first one.
- **How to score:** 5 if the founder still covers debt service after a 50% revenue drop and the deal is a small slice of net worth; 3 if it survives a 20% drop only; 1 if a single customer, license, piece of equipment, or person can sink it and nothing in the structure protects against that.
- **Quote:** "in every deal you do you need to find the one thing that could kill you" (S027)
- **Sources:** 8 (S001, S005, S007, S010, S011, S024, S026, S027)

## 6. Business or job test
- **What it is:** If the business needs you to run, you bought or built a job, not a business. She uses it both to judge targets and to judge the founder's own company.
- **Parts:** The three functions test: if fulfillment, sales, or distribution rests entirely on you, you are self-employed (S002). The vacation test: leave for two weeks and see whether money stops (S016); she also uses two to eight weeks plus "who here is better than you" to spot key man risk and a "sea of assistants" (S004). As a buy signal, the one month policy: the owner took a month off and it ran (S013). The bottleneck question: what system must exist so it does not matter who leaves (S031). The trap is the hero habit, which she calls heroin (S002, S004, S016).
- **When to use:** Valuing a target, deciding whether a founder can add a second business, or diagnosing why growth stalled.
- **How to score:** High if the business runs two weeks without the owner and someone inside is better than the owner at a core function; low if revenue tracks the owner's hours.
- **Quote:** "If any one of those three falls entirely in your hands, you're still self-employed." (S002)
- **Sources:** 8 (S002, S004, S013, S014, S016, S027, S031, S035)

## 7. I buy realities, not dreams (the four buy rules)
- **What it is:** Her pass filter. A target must be real, profitable, not a turnaround, and simple enough to explain to her grandmother.
- **Parts:** The four tests (S012). Her first-deal target profile: under $10M revenue (less private equity competition), blue-collar service, fragmented industry, at least a 15% margin, no proprietary or patent-pending tech (S020). Her image for it is a nice house on a normal block: average and profitable, not the best and not a fixer-upper, because something will go wrong in the first deal and you need cash flow cushion (S011).
- **When to use:** Screening any acquisition, and judging any plan that depends on a future that has not happened yet.
- **How to score:** Pass if any of the four tests fails. For a first deal, a margin under 15% or a turnaround is a pass.
- **Quote:** "I buy realities not dreams they have to be profitable businesses I don't do turnarounds" (S012)
- **Sources:** 7 (S010, S011, S012, S020, S021, S022, S027)

## 8. Personal P&L review and the Venmo challenge
- **What it is:** The businesses worth owning are already in your spending. Turn what you pay into what you own.
- **Parts:** List every small business you pay (Venmo, PayPal, credit card, bank statements, and your company's own vendors); drop the big companies; note what they do, what you spend, rough revenue, a value-add idea, and whether you would want to own it; check you can reach the owner; then have the owner conversation and make an offer, which can be a zero-dollar equity-for-growth deal (S001, S008, S020, S022). For a company, read your own P&L and ask which vendors to buy (S010). She says BizScout came from a problem she was paying to solve (S035).
- **When to use:** A buyer who does not know where to start, or a founder about to build a tool or service they already pay someone else for.
- **How to score:** High if the founder already pays a small, reachable, under-run vendor that does what they were about to build; low if the "build" has no existing provider to buy.
- **Quote:** "It's you turning your costs into profits." (S020)
- **Sources:** 7 (S001, S008, S010, S020, S022, S023, S035)

## 9. Price and terms
- **What it is:** A deal has only two levers. Amateurs fight over price; pros win on terms, which change the real price.
- **Parts:** She pays 3x to 5x profit as a ceiling and aims for 2x to 3x through structure (S013). Use seller notes, holdbacks, earn-outs, and escrow because the seller knows more (S010, S027). Compare the seller's P&L with the tax returns and settle in between (S010, S014). Read definitions: a clause paying a partner on gross instead of net nearly bankrupted her (S007). Her teaching stunt: offering a billion for a $10M business at a dollar a day (S007).
- **When to use:** Negotiating any purchase, sale, partnership, or comp plan.
- **How to score:** High if the structure lets the business pay for itself and shifts unverified risk back to the seller; low if the founder is anchored on headline price and ignoring payment timing, interest, gross versus net, and guarantees.
- **Quote:** "Anybody who focuses on price is an amateur. Pros focus on cash flow and terms." (S021)
- **Sources:** 7 (S007, S010, S013, S014, S018, S021, S027)

## 10. Courting the seller: curiosity, empathy, pain pushing, solution giving
- **What it is:** A seller conversation run like dating. Build trust, find the pain the owner already has, then offer a low-pressure way out. You cannot create pain, only find the owner who already wants out.
- **Parts:** Ask about the owner's history; show respect for what they built; probe the pain (staff, years worked, no successor); offer a solution (S008). Never arrive with a list of fixes (S008); an owner once answered her with "listen kid" (S018). Start with humility (S012); make the first meeting a conversation, not an interrogation (S023). Sellers care about certainty of closing and legacy, not only price (S020, S026). Make every conversation a win for the owner, even when you pass, because owners talk (S008).
- **When to use:** Off-market outreach, seller financing, and any negotiation with an owner who built the thing.
- **How to score:** High if the buyer can state the seller's real motive (retirement, burnout, legacy) and offers to solve it; low if the pitch is a lowball and a list of what is wrong.
- **Quote:** "you just start with curiosity empathy pain pushing solution solving" (S008)
- **Sources:** 7 (S008, S012, S014, S018, S020, S023, S026)

## 11. Off-market origination (quiet sellers and Rolodex to ROI)
- **What it is:** Most good deals are never listed. Find owners before they list, through the people and places that touch them.
- **Parts:** Tell everyone you buy businesses; work the two A's (attorneys and accountants), later four (plus insurance agents and advisors) (S022, S023); go to industry consultants, trade associations, and equipment distributors; go where owners already are (chambers of commerce, Rotary, clubs); use social media and library business directories; pay third-party sourcers a 2% to 3% success fee (S022). Pick one to three methods, not all twelve (S018, S022). On-market is faster and pricier; off-market is slower and usually cheaper (S018, S022). Her content exists partly to pull in deal flow (S005, S022, S028).
- **When to use:** Building a pipeline, or when a buyer complains there are no good deals.
- **How to score:** High if the founder has a repeatable source that produces owners who are not yet listed; low if they rely only on listings and brokers.
- **Where her view moved:** In 2023 she said she typically did not do on-market deals (S010); by 2025 her playbook teaches on-market search through her own marketplace, BizScout, alongside off-market (S020). Newer view: use both, off-market for price.
- **Quote:** "the one who turns over the most rocks wins" (S020)
- **Sources:** 7 (S005, S010, S018, S020, S022, S023, S028)

## 12. Micro private equity: buy, add value, exit higher
- **What it is:** Private equity's playbook shrunk to Main Street. Tiny profitable businesses nobody wants become worth more after value add, a roll-up, or a higher exit multiple.
- **Parts:** The roll-up recipe: buy several, clean them up, put them under one brand, add subscriptions, add tech, raise prices, add higher-level services (S010). Buy at about 2x cash flow, grow profit, sell at 3x to 4x (S010). Exit levers: raise profit, raise revenue, lower risk, add systems, processes, and people; at 3x to 5x profit, moving profit from $100K to $200K roughly doubles the sale price (S014). A private equity mentor's version: put systems in each business, find the one or two problems, fix them, sell on good terms (S004).
- **When to use:** Deciding what to do in the first years after buying, or whether a cheap business is cheap for a fixable reason.
- **How to score:** High if the plan names specific levers (price, recurring revenue, tech, a second unit) with numbers; low if the plan is "run it the same and hope."
- **Quote:** "And all it is is private equity 1 0 1 done at the micro level, done as at the tiny level." (S010)
- **Sources:** 7 (S004, S006, S007, S010, S013, S014, S023)

## 13. Buy, who, how and strategic acquisitions
- **What it is:** A problem-solving ladder. Poorer thinking asks how to fix a problem, better thinking asks who can fix it, and the best asks how to buy a nearly guaranteed solution.
- **Parts:** If you already run a business, buy strategically: a competitor, a talent source, a vendor, or a firm that lacks what you have, because you understand it and can raise its revenue (S014). Existing owners make the best acquirers (S011). Buy from your own P&L and own vendors your other companies use (S010, S022). Buy businesses that make each other better (S035). Her pack-and-ship example: the first store took 18 months to reach profit from scratch, the second was bought with customers and upsold (S006).
- **When to use:** A founder who wants a new capability, product, or channel and plans to build it in-house.
- **How to score:** High if a small company already does the thing and the founder's business would make it worth more; low if the acquisition is unrelated diversification by someone whose skill is not finance.
- **Quote:** "I have a problem how could I buy an almost guaranteed solution to this problem buy who how" (S014)
- **Sources:** 7 (S006, S010, S011, S014, S022, S023, S035)

## 14. Calling versus cash flow *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Split the decision by motive. If you cannot sleep for wanting to build a specific thing, start it. If you want income and freedom, treat it as a numbers problem and buy.
- **Parts:** Name the motive; if it is cash, compare the paths numerically. Her figures: a startup costs $20K to $100K over the first three years and the average founder earns about $40K a year in the first ten (S007); winners pay with three to four years of little income (S002). If the goal is $200K to $1M a year rather than changing the world, a startup is a poor path (S006). Build big things by choice, not necessity (S004).
- **When to use:** At the very start of a build-or-buy question, before any spreadsheet.
- **How to score:** Build scores high only if the founder is obsessed with this specific product and can fund years of low pay; otherwise score toward buying or buying in.
- **Quote:** "sleep for the want of it then do that that should be your startup if you just want cash flow this is the path for you" (S015)
- **Sources:** 6 (S002, S004, S006, S007, S015, S016)

## 15. SOWS: Stale, Old, Weak, Simple (with the fax machine test and Lindy)
- **What it is:** Her first-buy screen. Stale means un-innovated; Old means it has survived; Weak means weak competitors and a weak current owner; Simple means easy to understand.
- **Parts:** Fax machine test: a fax machine, a landline in use, or paper invoices means room to add software, marketing, and a team (S013, S014). Lindy effect: the longer a business has existed, the likelier it continues (S014, S021, S023). Her "old" ranges from 3 to 6 years (S018) to 5 to 7 (S014) to 5 to 10 (S011). Add-backs: owners run personal spending through the business, so reported profit understates real profit and lowers the price (S013).
- **When to use:** Picking a first acquisition, or judging whether a target has upside you can add.
- **How to score:** One point each for stale, old (5 years or more), weak competition, and simple. 4 is her ideal first deal; 2 or less is a pass for a beginner.
- **Quote:** "I look for a business that is stale that means that it's been around for a long time" (S014)
- **Sources:** 6 (S011, S013, S014, S018, S021, S023)

## 16. Gateway drug businesses and the levels of the game
- **What it is:** Start with a simple, small, local business (laundromat, small car wash, vending, a small service firm) to learn the P&L, selling, and deals without betting the house. Then sell it and move up.
- **Parts:** Her four levels from Main Street Millionaire: Solo Venture (owner-operator, gateway businesses), Hands-On CEO (a GM beside you), On-Deck Operator (an operator who knows more than you, $1M+ profit, roll-ups start), Market Leader (CEO and C-suite) (S014). The step up depends on whether revenue pays for the people who let you work on the business, not in it (S014). Private equity's version: level 1 to 10 games and operators (S001).
- **When to use:** Sizing a first deal, or telling a capable buyer they are aiming too small.
- **How to score:** For a first-time buyer, a gateway business scores high; for someone with real capital or skill it scores low, because the upside is capped (one laundromat pays about an employee's wage, per S005).
- **Quote:** "I call these gateway drug businesses so basically no if you're a billionaire you shouldn't go buy laundromat this is a terrible idea" (S005)
- **Sources:** 6 (S001, S005, S010, S012, S013, S014)

## 17. The seven Ds and the seller avatar
- **What it is:** Why owners sell, and which owners will sell to you on good terms.
- **Parts:** The seven Ds: departure, divorce, disease, disagreement, distress, death, dullness (S020, S023). The good seller wants to retire, wants more than a bank will lend, wants an income stream, runs a simple boring business, has no successor, and knows the buyer; the bad seller is young, growing fast, has many offers, and runs a complex business (S020, S022). The average owner is 65 or older (S001, S015, S022). Many owners do not know their business can be sold: her uncle closed a profitable plumbing company instead (S020).
- **When to use:** Prioritizing outreach and predicting whether creative terms are possible.
- **How to score:** Count avatar checks; more checks means a likelier seller and better terms. A young, fast-growing seller with multiple bids scores low.
- **Quote:** "We're going to go with old, tired, slow growing businesses, not fast, young, hungry businesses." (S020)
- **Sources:** 6 (S001, S008, S015, S020, S022, S023)

## 18. The Own system: P&L first, then the 12 profit levers (with the Owner Score)
- **What it is:** From Own or Be Owned. Run your business the way private equity runs a portfolio company: diagnose, score, set a strategy, and review every quarter, starting with where the money is hiding.
- **Parts:** Start with the P&L (where is this business hiding money), then fix pricing and how you get paid, then a growth roadmap (S004). The core levers are pricing, product, persona, profit, process, and people (S004); Promote is another of the twelve (S041). Three steps to more money: sell to richer customers, raise prices, add recurring revenue (S004). Track two top metrics per unit (S002). The Owner Score sorts owners into 12 archetypes (closer, ball hog, visionary, artist, martyr, workhorse, and others) that predict where they win and fail, such as artists underpricing (S002, S004, S016). Her claim: of about 1,600 businesses she reviewed, most were underpriced and under 30% had recurring revenue (S004).
- **When to use:** Before a founder builds or buys anything new. Fix the existing business first.
- **How to score:** Low if the founder wants a new product while prices have not risen, costs are unreviewed, and there is no recurring option; high if those are done and the next lever is clearly growth.
- **Quote:** "But the ones that matter are pricing, product, persona, profit, process, people." (S004)
- **Sources:** 6 (S002, S004, S016, S023, S025, S041)

## 19. Pre-sell before you build *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Her test for anyone who still wants to start. Get money, not compliments: pre-orders, deposits, or investors, before you spend on the build.
- **Parts:** Ask a few people to pay and take their cards (S004); see whether investors or pre-orders show up (S012); expect the friends who praised the idea to refuse to pay (S012). Her own proof runs: a window cleaning test to show a $100K business needs about $384 a day (S001); a trash route that pre-sold before the truck was bought (S011, S025). Her own failures Threads Refined and Selling South were never market tested (S004).
- **When to use:** Any build decision, new product, or new location.
- **How to score:** 5 if paying customers or deposits exist before the build; 3 if there are signed letters of intent only; 1 if the evidence is praise, likes, or a waitlist.
- **Quote:** "go and ask three people if they'd pay for it. Then take their credit cards and see if they'll pay for it." (S004)
- **Sources:** 5 (S001, S004, S011, S012, S025)

## 20. The deal funnel and reps (100 to one)
- **What it is:** Dealmaking is a numbers game. You look at many businesses to buy one, and fluency comes from reps.
- **Parts:** 100 to one (S001); about 50 listings to 20 analyzed to 5 letters of intent to 3 deep diligences to 1 bought (S020); 100, 50, 10, 1 (S023); 35 deals in one vertical for one seller-financed deal (S022). Track every deal through outreach, NDA, financials, assessment, diligence, offer, and close, with the reason a deal was killed (S020, S022). Timeline: 3 months to learn, 6 to close, 12 to integrate in 2023 (S027); 3 to learn, 9 to find and close, 12 to stabilize in 2024 (S023). Her company newsletter teaches the same rep idea (S038, team voice).
- **When to use:** A buyer who is stuck on one deal, falling in love with it, or giving up after a few nos.
- **How to score:** High if the buyer has reviewed dozens of deals and walked away from many; low if this is the first or second business they looked at.
- **Quote:** "the average buyer looks at about 30 businesses before making an offer." (S022)
- **Sources:** 5 (S001, S020, S022, S023, S027)

## 21. Income stream test and debt sizing
- **What it is:** The business's income has to carry the deal, not your hopes. If it cannot pay for itself and you, you bought a job.
- **Parts:** The income must cover interest on the debt, the income you need, an operator's salary, and working capital (S014). Keep leverage at or under about 30% on a first deal (S021); aim for a 3 to 5 year payback on money down (S010); buy below what the bank approves and keep cash after closing (S026).
- **Where her view moved:** In 2023 she said she would never do a personally guaranteed loan on first deals (S027). By 2026 she teaches SBA loans, which can require a personal guarantee, while warning that the SBA can come after everything you own (S021, S026). Newer view: guarantees are acceptable with eyes open and conservative sizing.
- **When to use:** Any purchase with debt.
- **How to score:** 5 if cash flow covers all four buckets with margin after a stress case; 3 if it covers debt and a modest owner income only; 1 if the buyer needs refinancing or growth to make the payments.
- **Quote:** "you don't buy a job you buy a business" (S014)
- **Sources:** 5 (S010, S014, S021, S026, S027)

## 22. Rent Check and the Ad Trap
- **What it is:** Paid growth can outrun real economics: it rewards you at first, then acquisition cost climbs while margins fall. Own your demand rather than rent it.
- **Parts:** Profit = (LTV minus CAC) times customers. Three numbers to pull this week: Ad Dependency Ratio (paid revenue over total revenue), real profit after ads, cost of goods, and fixed costs over three months, and the 12-month CAC trend. Media ladder: owned first, earned second, paid last as amplification. ROCS (return on content spend) prices the relationship where ROAS prices the transaction (S041). Fix the product before turning up ads; a leads problem is usually a product problem (S001). Audience is the one form of leverage you can build without permission (S005, S011).
- **When to use:** A founder whose growth depends on paid ads, or who wants to build a channel versus buy reach.
- **How to score:** Her thresholds: Ad Dependency under 30% healthy, 30% to 60% danger, over 60% trapped (S041). She says these are rules of thumb and diversifying by $5M to $10M is the owner's call.
- **Quote:** "Under 30% is healthy. 30% to 60% is danger. Over 60% and you're already in the trap." (S041)
- **Sources:** 5 (S001, S005, S011, S017, S041)

## 23. The first 90 days: transfer and transition
- **What it is:** After closing, change almost nothing at first. Document, shadow the seller, then stabilize, then grow.
- **Parts:** Days 0 to 60 transfer and transition (document everything, follow the owner around), 60 to 90 stabilize and systematize, 90 to 120 power up, 120 plus harness (S023). It is the last of her ten buying steps (S007, S022). She runs her businesses on 90-day sprints with 30-day check-ins (S002).
- **When to use:** Any acquisition plan that promises big changes right after close.
- **How to score:** High if the plan holds changes until the business is documented and the seller's knowledge has transferred; low if the buyer plans to rebuild in month one.
- **Quote:** "the first 60 days are called transfer and transition so this is where you're documenting everything you're following the person around and you're really not changing much" (S023)
- **Sources:** 4 (S002, S007, S022, S023)

## 24. BRRT: Boring, Recession resistant, Raise prices, add Technology
- **What it is:** Her company's buy box in four letters. She sends back anything that does not fit.
- **Parts:** Boring industries; recession-resistant demand; prices that can rise because the business is usually underpriced; technology to add. Then repeat by adding more of the same (S011). She turned her rules into named frameworks because she has a bad memory and otherwise does bad deals (S008).
- **When to use:** A fast first screen on any target.
- **How to score:** One point per letter. A business that cannot raise prices or add tech has no value-add story and scores low even if it is boring.
- **Quote:** "we buy boring businesses in recession resistant asset classes raise their prices and add technology" (S011)
- **Sources:** 2 (S008, S011)

## 25. Lifestyle, scale, or exit
- **What it is:** A business can optimize for one of three things, and you have to pick one for at least a year.
- **Parts:** Lifestyle (freedom and pay), scale (growth, with less cash out), exit (built to sell for the best price) (S016). The business is like a baby: life support in year one, walking in year two, motoring in year three, self-sufficient by years four to seven, so do not expect freedom early (S016). By return profile: bond-like businesses at 3% to 7% with little work, minor-growth businesses at 10% to 20% a year, and high-speed tech (S012). Before choosing, write how much risk, money, time, and sacrifice you will give (S016). She ran Contrarian Thinking as lifestyle for two years, then switched it to scale (S016).
