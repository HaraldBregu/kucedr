# Frameworks: Jeff Bezos

24 frameworks, most-used first. "Sources" counts distinct sources in the corpus that use it (S002/S044 and S026/S030 count once). Frameworks marked *(Invent and Wander)* also appear in his collected letters book, which reprints the shareholder letters.

---

## 1. Type 1 and Type 2 decisions (one-way and two-way doors) *(Invent and Wander)*
- **What it is:** Sort a decision by how hard it is to reverse, then match the amount of process to that.
- **Parts:** Type 1 (one-way door): consequential and irreversible or nearly so; decide slowly, with consultation, and look for one more way to analyze it. Type 2 (two-way door): reversible; a single high-judgment person or small team decides fast, and backs up if wrong. Senior leaders set the tone so juniors do not use heavy process on trivial calls.
- **When to use:** Every decision, first. Especially when a growing company is slowing down.
- **How to score:** Reversibility 1 to 5 and consequence 1 to 5. One-way and high consequence: slow down, add analysis, raise to the founder. Anything reversible: decide this week with one owner. Flag "one-size-fits-all" process as a Day 2 symptom.
- **Quote:** "Some decisions are consequential and irreversible or nearly irreversible - one-way doors - and these decisions must be made methodically, carefully, slowly, with great deliberation and consultation." (S063)
- **Sources:** 7 (S003, S007, S024, S025, S045, S063, S064)

## 2. Regret minimization framework
- **What it is:** For a big personal or career leap, project yourself to age 80 and pick the path that leaves the fewest regrets.
- **Parts:** Picture yourself at 80 (or 90) looking back. Most regrets are acts of omission. Short-term costs, like a lost bonus, fade. Trying and failing is rarely regretted.
- **When to use:** Leaving a safe job, starting a company, any personal one-way door. He used it for Amazon in 1994 after a 48-hour pause.
- **How to score:** Ask whether the founder would be haunted at 80 by not trying (paraphrase of S043). Yes means the leap scores high on this lens. It does not price business risk; pair it with the door test and runway math.
- **Quote:** "When I'm 80 and reflecting back, I want to have minimized the number of regrets that I have in my life." (S041)
- **Sources:** 8 (S004, S005, S020, S024, S041, S043, S044, S047)

## 3. Day 1 versus Day 2 *(Invent and Wander)*
- **What it is:** Day 1 is startup vitality at any size. Day 2 is the slide into decline.
- **Parts:** Day 2 runs stasis, irrelevance, decline, death. The Day 1 defense: true customer obsession, a skeptical view of proxies (process, surveys, metrics), eager adoption of external trends, and high-velocity decision making.
- **When to use:** Diagnosing a company that has grown slow, process-bound, or inward-looking.
- **How to score:** Check the four defenses. "We followed the process" as an excuse, decisions waiting for 90 percent information, or fighting a big trend all score as Day 2.
- **Quote:** "Day 2 is stasis. Followed by irrelevance. Followed by excruciating, painful decline. Followed by death. And that is why it is always Day 1." (S064)
- **Sources:** 9 (S003, S004, S024, S042, S044, S045, S057, S064, S068)

## 4. What's not going to change
- **What it is:** Build strategy on customer needs that will hold for ten years, not on predictions of change.
- **Parts:** Ask what will not change. Name the stable needs (for retail: low prices, fast delivery, vast selection; for AWS: reliability, low cost, fast API innovation). Pour energy into them for years, because the investment keeps paying.
- **When to use:** Setting long-term strategy in a fast-changing market, including AI.
- **How to score:** Run the absurdity test: would any customer ever wish you were slower, worse, or pricier (paraphrase of the S010 line in `_hot.md`)? If no, it is a stable need and a good place to invest.
- **Quote:** "I almost never get the question what's not going to change in the next 10 years and I submit to you that that second question is actually the more important of the two" (S029)
- **Sources:** 10 (S010, S011, S014, S023, S024, S029, S031, S036, S045, S056)

## 5. Start with the customer and work backwards *(Invent and Wander)*
- **What it is:** Begin from a customer need and build whatever skills it takes, instead of asking what else your current skills can do.
- **Parts:** Find a need that is meaningful and durable. Commit to it for years. Hire or learn the missing skill (Kindle needed hardware engineers). Use "skills forward" (AWS came from internal skills) as a complement, never the only method.
- **When to use:** Choosing new products and businesses.
- **How to score:** Is the starting point a customer need or a capability we want to use? If the vision shrank to fit current skills, score it down.
- **Quote:** "Working backwards from customer needs often demands that we acquire new competencies and exercise new muscles, never mind how uncomfortable and awkward-feeling those first steps might be." (S056)
- **Sources:** 7 (S008, S009, S018, S028, S029, S056, S057)

## 6. Customer obsession: listen, invent, personalize
- **What it is:** His precise definition of customer-centric.
- **Parts:** Listen to customers. Invent on their behalf, because they cannot invent for themselves (Prime, AWS, and Echo were never requested). Personalize so each customer is at the center of their own store.
- **When to use:** Product strategy, and whenever a team is tempted to follow requests literally or watch rivals.
- **How to score:** Does the plan only listen (incremental), or does it also invent something customers do not know to ask for? Competitor-driven plans score low.
- **Quote:** "Customer obsession is not just listening to customers. Customer obsession is also inventing on their behalf because it's not their job to invent for themselves." (S014)
- **Sources:** 8 (S009, S014, S021, S027, S041, S057, S064, S066)

## 7. The flywheel (cost, price, growth) *(Invent and Wander)*
- **What it is:** A self-reinforcing loop where lower costs fund lower prices, prices drive volume, and volume spreads fixed costs.
- **Parts:** Cut defects and cost. Lower prices. Grow volume. Spread fixed costs. Repeat. Later version: selection and price attract customers, customers attract sellers, sellers add selection, and FBA ties Marketplace to Prime.
- **When to use:** Businesses with high fixed costs and price-sensitive customers; designing offers that feed each other.
- **How to score:** Does each part make another part stronger? A new offer that only adds revenue, without feeding the loop, scores low.
- **Quote:** "Focus on cost improvement makes it possible for us to afford to lower prices, which drives growth." (S049)
- **Sources:** 7 (S010, S040, S049, S051, S062, S063, S022)

## 8. Controllable inputs, not outputs *(Invent and Wander)*
- **What it is:** Manage the inputs a team can control; financial results follow.
- **Parts:** Name the output (profit, stock price). Work backwards through its drivers until you reach something a team controls (selection growth, defects, delivery speed). Give each input an owner, deliverable, and date. Review several times a year.
- **When to use:** Goal setting and team ownership.
- **How to score:** Count how many goals are inputs. Goals stated as revenue or margin for a small team score low.
- **Quote:** "if you focus on the controllable inputs to your business instead of the outputs in the long term you get better results" (S018)
- **Sources:** 4 (S011, S018, S057, S061)

## 9. Free cash flow per share and the NPV test *(Invent and Wander)*
- **What it is:** The financial measure he wants to maximize, over earnings, EPS, or EBITDA.
- **Parts:** Operating cash, minus capex, minus working capital needs, divided by shares. Run NPV on growth that needs capital: earnings can grow while value is destroyed. Manage dilution.
- **When to use:** Capital-heavy growth, pricing, and any choice between looking good on GAAP and cash.
- **How to score:** Does the plan raise long-term free cash flow per share? If capex outruns the present value of the cash it produces, growth hurts owners.
- **Quote:** "shares are worth only the present value of their future cash flows, not the present value of their future earnings." (S052)
- **Sources:** 5 (S038, S050, S052, S056, S068)

## 10. Math-based versus judgment-based decisions *(Invent and Wander)*
- **What it is:** Let data decide when history can model the answer; use judgment when it cannot.
- **Parts:** Math-based: enough history, so judgment is a junior partner (inventory, capacity). Judgment-based: little data and the experiment would amount to deciding (Prime, price cuts, third-party sellers). When anecdotes and metrics disagree, the anecdotes are usually right and the metric is suspect.
- **When to use:** Before any big call, to choose how to decide.
- **How to score:** Can a cheap test settle it? Then test. If not, name the judgment, accept controversy, and check data against intuition.
- **Quote:** "Math-based decisions command wide agreement, whereas judgment-based decisions are rightly debated and often controversial, at least until put into practice and demonstrated." (S053)
- **Sources:** 7 (S003, S011, S023, S024, S044, S045, S053)

## 11. High-velocity decisions (the 70 percent rule) *(Invent and Wander)*
- **What it is:** Keep decision quality and speed together.
- **Parts:** Never use one-size-fits-all process. Decide most things with about 70 percent of the information you wish you had. Be good at spotting and fixing bad decisions. Disagree and commit. Spot true misalignment early and escalate it.
- **When to use:** Any organization where decisions stall.
- **How to score:** Is the team waiting for 90 percent? Is a dispute being settled by stamina? Both are speed failures.
- **Quote:** "most decisions should probably be made with somewhere around 70% of the information you wish you had. If you wait for 90%, in most cases, you're probably being slow." (S064)
- **Sources:** 5 (S007, S025, S045, S063, S064)

## 12. Disagree and commit *(Invent and Wander)*
- **What it is:** End a standoff without compromise or attrition: state the disagreement, then back the decision fully.
- **Parts:** Say plainly that you disagree. Gamble on the person closer to the ground truth. No sniping or "I told you so". Help it work. It applies to the boss too.
- **When to use:** When no one can know the answer and consensus would take too long.
- **How to score:** After the call, is everyone helping it work? Lingering second-guessing scores as a failure of the mechanism.
- **Quote:** "I want you to gamble with me on this because I don't know if it's right either." (S036)
- **Sources:** 3 (S036, S045, S064)

## 13. Long-tailed bets and the right kind of failure *(Invent and Wander)*
- **What it is:** Business payoffs are uncapped, unlike baseball, so take many bets with low odds and huge upside, and grow the size of failures with the company.
- **Parts:** A true experiment has an unknown outcome. Failing at operations you already know is not a good failure. Big winners pay for many losers. Salvage people and lessons (Fire phone fed Echo). Never bet the company.
- **When to use:** Deciding how much risk to take and whether a failure deserves tolerance.
- **How to score:** Expected value with capped downside. Is the downside survivable? Is it a real experiment or sloppy execution?
- **Quote:** "If you know it's gonna work in advance it is not an experiment." (S025)
- **Sources:** 9 (S014, S018, S025, S036, S041, S044, S061, S063, S066)

## 14. The last high-judgment champion
- **What it is:** His rule for when to kill a struggling bet.
- **Parts:** Keep going while at least one person with good judgment still believes. When the last one folds, stop. Give up on things, but not easily.
- **When to use:** Multi-year projects that have not paid off.
- **How to score:** Name the champions. If none remain, or the founder doubts his own judgment, stop.
- **Quote:** "I think it's when the last High judgment champion folds his or her cards." (S010)
- **Sources:** 2 (S010, S036)

## 15. Wandering and efficiency *(Invent and Wander)*
- **What it is:** Two working modes. Execute efficiently when you know the destination; wander, guided by hunch and curiosity, when you do not.
- **Parts:** Efficiency for known paths. Wandering for non-linear discoveries no customer asked for (AWS, Echo). Conviction that the prize is big enough to justify the mess.
- **When to use:** Splitting a team's time between exploring and executing.
- **How to score:** Is the destination known? If yes, demand a plan. If no, protect room to wander.
- **Quote:** "Wandering is an essential counter-balance to efficiency. You need to employ both." (S066)
- **Sources:** 6 (S022, S023, S024, S042, S044, S066)

## 16. Four elements of high standards *(Invent and Wander)*
- **What it is:** How to build high standards in a team.
- **Parts:** Standards are teachable, domain specific, need recognition of what good looks like, and need realistic scope (how hard it will be).
- **When to use:** When quality is uneven or work disappoints.
- **How to score:** Diagnose a miss: did the team not recognize good, or did they underestimate the effort? Most misses are scope.
- **Quote:** "So, the four elements of high standards as we see it: they are teachable, they are domain specific, you must recognize them, and you must explicitly coach realistic scope." (S065)
- **Sources:** 1 (S065)

## 17. Six-page narrative memo
- **What it is:** Replace slides with a narrative memo everyone reads in silence at the start of the meeting.
- **Parts:** Six pages of real sentences. About 30 minutes of silent study hall. The most junior person speaks first, the most senior last. Crisp document, messy meeting. A great memo takes a week or more.
- **When to use:** Any important decision meeting.
- **How to score:** Was there a written narrative that forced clear thinking? Was dissent surfaced?
- **Quote:** "We don't do PowerPoint (or any other slide-oriented) presentations at Amazon." (S065)
- **Sources:** 4 (S001, S003, S045, S065)

## 18. New business tests and the dreamy business *(Invent and Wander)*
- **What it is:** Screens before putting money into a new line.
- **Parts:** Can it earn the returns on capital owners expect? Can it grow big enough to matter? Is the opportunity underserved? Do we bring strong customer-facing differentiation? Dreamy version: customers love it, it can get very large, strong returns on capital, durable for decades.
- **When to use:** Entering a new market or product line.
- **How to score:** One point per test. He resisted physical stores for years because they failed all but the size test (S054).
- **Quote:** "A dreamy business offering has at least four characteristics. Customers love it, it can grow to very large size, it has strong returns on capital, and it's durable in time - with the potential to endure for decades." (S062)
- **Sources:** 4 (S028, S036, S054, S062)

## 19. Price of admission and heavy-lifting infrastructure
- **What it is:** Startup booms happen once heavy infrastructure exists; where it does not, building it is the highest-leverage move.
- **Parts:** Amazon rode the postal system, credit cards, and the phone network. Space lacks that layer, so Blue Origin builds reusable launch. Find the real cost driver (discarded hardware, not fuel).
- **When to use:** Judging whether a market is open to small players, and whether to build or ride infrastructure.
- **How to score:** What existing infrastructure can this ride? If none, is the entry price too high to try?
- **Quote:** "Today you cannot start a significant space company in your dorm room, it's impossible. The price of admission is too high." (S035)
- **Sources:** 10 (S010, S013, S014, S016, S017, S022, S030, S031, S035, S045)

## 20. Undifferentiated heavy lifting
- **What it is:** Separate the work that makes your product special from work that must be done well but does not differentiate, and rent the second as a utility.
- **Parts:** The brewery that made its own electricity. Data centers as today's generators. Reserve custom builds for real special sauce.
- **When to use:** Build-versus-buy calls.
- **How to score:** Does doing this in-house make the "beer taste better"? If not, buy it.
- **Quote:** "The undifferentiated heavy lifting by the way has to be done at World Class levels of Excellence or your vision will fail" (S012)
- **Sources:** 4 (S001, S012, S025, S028)

## 21. Mirror test for critics
- **What it is:** A procedure for criticism of a new move.
- **Parts:** Look in the mirror. If critics are right, change. If you made a mistake, apologize. If you still believe you are right, hold firm. Separate well-meaning critics from self-interested incumbents.
- **When to use:** Public or internal pushback on something new.
- **How to score:** Did we honestly assess the critique before deciding to change or hold?
- **Quote:** "when you look in the mirror, assess the criticism, and still believe you're doing the right thing, no force in the world should be able to move you." (S041)
- **Sources:** 4 (S003, S010, S041, S047)

## 22. Systematic risk elimination
- **What it is:** What early capital is for.
- **Parts:** Be honest about the odds (he gave Amazon about 30 percent). List the biggest risks. Spend early dollars removing them one at a time. Take bigger risks once the company is robust.
- **When to use:** Seed-stage spending and fundraising.
- **How to score:** Does this spend remove a top risk, or add a new one?
- **Quote:** "What startup companies do is they take their precious early capital dollars and systematically eliminate risks." (S008)
- **Sources:** 3 (S008, S029, S043)

## 23. Missionary versus mercenary
- **What it is:** A test for founders, teams, and acquisition targets.
- **Parts:** Missionaries love the product and customer; mercenaries want to flip the company. Missionaries build better products and usually make more money.
- **When to use:** Choosing what to start, whom to back, or whom to buy.
- **How to score:** Is the motive the product and customer, or the exit?
- **Quote:** "I would take a missionary over a mercenary any day" (S029)
- **Sources:** 6 (S028, S029, S036, S037, S044, S055)

## 24. Gates for taking on a business
- **What it is:** The tests he ran before buying The Washington Post.
- **Parts:** Is it important (would I do this if it were a boring business)? Is it hopeless? Is there a good team? Do I bring something it lacks (internet knowledge, runway)?
- **When to use:** A distressed or unfamiliar business, or an unplanned opportunity outside the core.
- **How to score:** Fail gate 1 or 2 and stop.
- **Quote:** "if this were a financially upside-down, salty snack-food company, the answer would be no." (S044)
- **Sources:** 4 (S003, S018, S044, S047)
